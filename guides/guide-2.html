NSW Food Safety Laws and Regulations 2026: What Every Food Business Must Know

Navigating food safety law in NSW can feel overwhelming. Between the Food Act 2003, the Food Regulation 2015, and the national FSANZ Food Standards Code, there is a lot to keep track of. Add recent changes like Standard 3.2.2A, and it is easy to see why so many business owners feel unsure about their compliance obligations.

This guide cuts through the complexity. Whether you run a bakery in Bondi, a food truck in Byron Bay, or a wholesale kitchen in Dubbo, here is exactly what NSW food safety law requires of you in 2026.

The Legislative Hierarchy: How NSW Food Law Works

Australian food regulation operates under a cooperative federal model. The FSANZ Food Standards Code sets the national standards. Each state and territory then adopts those standards into local legislation. In NSW, the relevant acts are:

Food Act 2003 (NSW)
This is the primary legislation governing food safety in NSW. It adopts the FSANZ Code as law in NSW and gives enforcement powers to the NSW Food Authority and local councils. Key requirements under the Act include:

  • All food sold in NSW must be safe and suitable for human consumption
  • Food businesses must be registered with their local council or the NSW Food Authority
  • Food handlers must have appropriate skills and knowledge (now reinforced under Standard 3.2.2A)
  • Offences include selling unsafe food, misleading labelling, and failing to maintain a food safety program

Food Regulation 2015 (NSW)
This regulation provides the operational details that support the Food Act. It covers food business notification, food safety supervisor requirements, and the specific offences and penalties that apply. Significant updates in recent years have strengthened requirements around allergen management, food handler training, and record-keeping for high-risk businesses.

FSANZ Food Standards Code
The Code is divided into four chapters. For most NSW food businesses, the most relevant standards are:

  • Standard 3.1.1 – Interpretation and Application (definitions)
  • Standard 3.2.1 – Food Safety Programs (requires a HACCP-based program)
  • Standard 3.2.2 – Food Safety Practices and General Requirements (temperature control, hygiene, cleaning)
  • Standard 3.2.2A – Food Safety Management Tools (added in 2023, now fully enforceable)
  • Standard 3.2.3 – Food Premises and Equipment (design, construction, maintenance)

The Role of the NSW Food Authority

The NSW Food Authority is the state government agency responsible for regulating the food industry. It handles:

  • Registration and inspection of food businesses deemed "high-risk" or operating across multiple council areas
  • Enforcement of the Food Act and Food Regulation
  • Food safety recalls and incident response
  • Education and guidance for industry
  • Accreditation of Food Safety Supervisors

If you run a single-site cafe or restaurant, your local council's Environmental Health Officer (EHO) is your primary regulator. They conduct routine inspections, respond to complaints, and enforce the same standards. The NSW Food Authority typically steps in for larger operations, manufacturers, or during serious incidents. Our guide to passing a council food inspection explains exactly what your local EHO will be looking for.

Standard 3.2.2A: The Game Changer

Introduced in December 2023 and now fully enforced across NSW, Standard 3.2.2A adds three mandatory food safety management tools for businesses that handle high-risk food:

  • Tool 1: Food Safety Management System – A documented HACCP-based system (see our HACCP guide and food safety program template guide)
  • Tool 2: Food Handler Training – All food handlers must have adequate skills and knowledge. At least one person per premises must hold a certified Food Safety Supervisor qualification from a registered training organisation
  • Tool 3: Allergen Management – A documented allergen management plan (covered in our allergen management guide)

This standard applies to food service, retail, and hospitality businesses that handle unpackaged, potentially hazardous food. If you slice a tomato, cook a steak, or prepare a sandwich, you are in scope. The transition period has now ended, and councils across NSW are actively enforcing these requirements.

Food Safety Supervisor Requirements

Under the Food Regulation 2015, certain businesses must nominate a Food Safety Supervisor (FSS). The FSS must:

  • Hold a nationally recognised FSS certificate from a Registered Training Organisation
  • Be reasonably available at all times the business operates
  • Oversee the day-to-day food safety operations
  • Be familiar with your HACCP-based food safety program

Registration and Notification Obligations

Every food business in NSW must be registered or notified with the appropriate authority. Here is how it breaks down:

  • Retail and food service businesses (cafes, restaurants, bakeries, takeaway) – register with your local council
  • Manufacturers, wholesalers, and importers – register with the NSW Food Authority
  • Community and charitable organisations – may need to notify, depending on the nature of food handling

Registration is not a one-time event. You must notify your council or the Authority of any changes to your business, such as a change of ownership, menu, or premises layout.

Penalties for Non-Compliance

NSW takes food safety seriously. Penalties under the Food Act 2003 include:

  • On-the-spot fines of up to $880 for individuals, $4,400 for corporations (for minor breaches)
  • Court-imposed penalties of up to $55,000 for individuals and $275,000 for corporations for serious offences
  • Prohibition orders that prevent individuals from managing food businesses
  • Closure orders for premises that pose an immediate risk to public health
  • Naming on the NSW Food Authority's "Name and Shame" register

The most common breaches we see in our internal audit work include poor temperature control, inadequate cleaning and sanitation, and lack of documented food safety programs. All of these are preventable with the right systems in place.

Labelling Requirements Under the Code

The FSANZ Code also regulates food labelling. Key requirements include:

  • Mandatory allergen declarations on packaged and unpackaged food
  • Date marking (use-by and best-before dates)
  • Nutrition information panels (for packaged food)
  • Country of origin labelling
  • Lot identification for traceability

If you package food on-site (e.g., a bakery selling wrapped pastries), these requirements apply. Allergen declarations are particularly important following Standard 3.2.2A. Our Allergen Management Plan Guide covers the labelling requirements in depth.

How to Stay Compliant in 2026

Compliance is not a destination; it is an ongoing process. Here is what we recommend to our clients:

  • Review your HACCP-based food safety program at least annually
  • Ensure your Food Safety Supervisor certification is current (renewal is required every 5 years)
  • Conduct regular internal audits to catch gaps before the council does
  • Keep your training records up to date every food handler needs documented evidence of their skills
  • Subscribe to NSW Food Authority updates to stay informed of regulatory changes
  • Download our free audit readiness checklist for a quick compliance self-assessment

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Food Act 2003 and the Food Regulation 2015?
The Food Act 2003 is the primary legislation passed by the NSW Parliament. It sets out the broad legal framework, including offences and enforcement powers. The Food Regulation 2015 is a subordinate instrument that provides the operational detail, such as specific registration requirements, Food Safety Supervisor mandates, and penalty amounts. Think of the Act as the "what" and the Regulation as the "how."
Does Standard 3.2.2A apply to my small takeaway shop?
If you handle unpackaged, potentially hazardous food, yes. A takeaway shop that cooks and serves hot food, prepares salads, or handles raw ingredients is required to implement the three food safety management tools: a documented food safety program, trained food handlers with an FSS, and an allergen management plan. Even if you only serve pre-packaged items, it is good practice to have these systems in place.
How do I know if I need to register with the NSW Food Authority or my local council?
As a general rule, if you sell food directly to consumers from a single premises (cafe, restaurant, takeaway, bakery), you register with your local council. If you manufacture, process, or wholesale food, or operate across multiple council areas, you register with the NSW Food Authority. When in doubt, contact the NSW Food Authority for guidance.
