Food Safety for Home Businesses and Cottage Food in NSW: A Complete Compliance Guide

Starting a food business from your home kitchen — often called a cottage food business — is an attractive pathway for many entrepreneurs in NSW. Whether you are baking sourdough, making jams, producing spice blends, or catering small events from home, the regulations are real and they apply to you. The Food Act 2003 (NSW) and Food Regulation 2015 (NSW) do not distinguish between a home-based food business and a commercial restaurant — the same food safety outcomes are required.

This guide covers everything you need to know to operate a home-based food business lawfully in NSW: council registration, food safety requirements, labelling obligations, what foods are and are not permitted from a home kitchen, and how to navigate the regulatory pathway without getting overwhelmed.

Do I need to register my home food business with council?

Yes. Under the Food Regulation 2015 (NSW), any person who sells food as part of a business must be registered or notified with the local council in the LGA where the food is prepared. This includes home-based businesses. The costs vary by council — typically between $80 and $350 per year for a Category 2 notification — but operating without registration exposes you to penalties, enforcement orders, and legal liability if a customer becomes ill.

To register, contact your local council's environmental health team. They will ask you to complete a food business notification form and may require:

  • A floor plan of your home kitchen showing the food preparation area, storage, and washing facilities
  • A copy of your proposed menu or product list
  • Evidence of your Food Safety Supervisor (FSS) certificate
  • A completed food safety program or declaration form (council-dependent)

The council EHO will usually conduct an inspection of your home kitchen before approving registration. This inspection is your opportunity to demonstrate that your home kitchen can operate to commercial food safety standards.

What foods are allowed from a home kitchen in NSW?

This is the most common question we receive from aspiring home-based food businesses. The answer depends on your kitchen setup, your menu, and your local council's risk assessment. In general, NSW councils allow lower-risk foods from home kitchens, including:

  • Baked goods (bread, cakes, biscuits, slices) that are shelf-stable
  • Jams, chutneys, and preserves with high sugar and acid content
  • Dried herbs, spice blends, and tea mixes
  • Confectionery and chocolates (non-tempered or with shelf-stable fillings)
  • Crystallised and dried fruit
  • Muesli, granola, and dry mixes

However, many NSW councils restrict or prohibit the preparation of potentially hazardous foods in a home kitchen, including:

  • Raw or cooked meat, poultry, and seafood products
  • Dairy-based products (cheese, yoghurt, custards, cream-filled goods)
  • Egg-based products (custard tarts, quiche, mayonnaise, mousse)
  • Cooked rice and pasta dishes for sale
  • Cut fresh fruit and vegetables for retail sale
  • Low-acid canned or bottled goods (soups, sauces without high acid/sugar)

Some councils may allow these higher-risk foods under strict conditions — for example, if you install a commercial-grade ventilation system, a separate hand-wash basin, and dedicated refrigeration — but in practice, most home kitchens cannot meet the infrastructure requirements for handling potentially hazardous food. Always check with your council before developing a menu.

Setting up your home kitchen for compliance

Your home kitchen must meet the same basic requirements as a commercial food premises under Standard 3.2.3 of the Food Standards Code, adapted for a residential setting. This means:

  • Food preparation surfaces must be smooth, impervious, and easy to clean — no wooden bench tops or cracked tiles
  • A dedicated hand-washing basin with warm running water, soap, and paper towels — separate from the dishwashing sink
  • Refrigeration that can maintain food below 5°C, with a thermometer placed in the warmest zone
  • Separate storage for food and non-food items — cleaning chemicals must be stored away from food ingredients
  • Adequate pest-proofing: flyscreens on windows, no gaps under doors, sealed rubbish bins
  • Good lighting and ventilation to prevent condensation and mould

Many home-based businesses designate a specific section of their kitchen as the "production zone" and keep it separate from family cooking. This is not a legal requirement but is strongly recommended — it demonstrates to the EHO that you take contamination risks seriously.

You must also maintain a cleaning schedule that covers all food-contact surfaces, floors, and walls in the preparation area. Document your cleaning procedures in a written SOP and keep a cleaning log that you can show during inspection.

Labelling requirements for home-based food businesses

When you sell food from home, your labels must comply with the Food Standards Code — there is no "cottage food exemption" in NSW. Standard 1.2.1 and 1.2.2 require that packaged food for retail sale include:

  • Name and business address of the manufacturer (your home address or a registered business address)
  • Name of the food (e.g., "Raspberry Jam", "Sourdough Loaf")
  • Ingredient list in descending order by weight
  • Date marking — use-by date for perishable items, best-before date for shelf-stable items
  • Weight or volume declaration
  • Country of origin (e.g., "Made in Australia from at least 95% Australian ingredients")
  • Allergen declaration — bold the nine declared allergens in the ingredient list
  • Nutrition information panel (NIP) — required unless your business qualifies for an exemption (e.g., very small businesses selling directly to consumers may be exempt, but check council policy)

If you sell at farmers' markets or online, the same labelling rules apply. Attaching a handwritten sticker to a jar is acceptable provided it contains all the required information and is legible. For online sales, display the full ingredient list and allergen information clearly in the product description.

Incorrect or missing allergen declarations are the most common compliance issue for home-based businesses and can lead to legal liability if a customer has an allergic reaction. Invest in professional label review or use our consulting service to audit your labels before printing.

Food Safety Supervisor requirement for home businesses

Yes, home-based food businesses in NSW must appoint a Food Safety Supervisor (FSS). The FSS must hold a current Statement of Attainment in the relevant unit — typically SITXFSA005 (Use hygienic practices for food safety) and SITXFSA006 (Participate in safe food handling practices) for most food sectors, or SIRRFSA001 for retail-specific operations.

The FSS can be you, the business owner. You do not need a separate employee to fill this role. The key requirement is that the FSS is "reasonably available" while the business is operating and is responsible for overseeing food safety practices. Complete your FSS certification through a registered training organisation (RTO) — it is a one-off investment that also strengthens your credibility with customers and retailers.

Selling at farmers' markets and online

Selling at farmers' markets requires additional considerations. Each market operator may require evidence of your council registration, FSS certification, and public liability insurance (typically $10–$20 million cover). The council where the market is held may also require you to register as a temporary food business in their LGA, even if you are already registered in your home council.

For online sales, including through social media platforms, you must include your business name and council registration number on your sales page or invoice. The same food safety obligations apply — if a customer receives food that has been temperature-abused during transport, you are responsible. Use insulated packaging, ice bricks, and express shipping for perishable items, and clearly state delivery window expectations on your website.

Home food business insurance

Standard home and contents insurance does not cover commercial food production. You need dedicated public and product liability insurance for your food business. This covers you if a customer claims illness, injury, or property damage from your product. Many market organisers and online platforms require proof of insurance before you can sell. Expect premiums in the range of $500–$1,500 per year depending on your menu, sales volume, and claims history.

What happens during a council inspection of a home kitchen?

The EHO will visit your home kitchen to verify that it meets the requirements of the Food Standards Code. They will inspect:

  • The condition of benches, floors, walls, and fixtures
  • Hand-washing facilities and soap/paper towel supply
  • Refrigeration temperature and thermometer placement
  • Storage of food, packaging, and chemicals — verifying separation
  • Pest-proofing measures (flyscreens, door seals)
  • Waste management and bin cleanliness
  • Your cleaning schedule, temperature logs, and FSS certificate

Be present and cooperative during the inspection. If the EHO identifies issues, they may issue a notice of non-compliance with a timeframe for rectification. Serious breaches — such as evidence of pest infestation, no hand-washing facilities, or unsafe food storage — can lead to immediate closure or suspension of your registration.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell food made in my home kitchen to cafes and restaurants in NSW?
It depends. Selling wholesale to other businesses (cafes, restaurants, retailers) places your products in a different supply chain and may trigger additional requirements under the Food Standards Code. Some councils treat this as a higher-risk activity and may require you to upgrade to a commercial kitchen. You must also provide your customers with accurate labels, shelf-life data, and allergen information. If you plan to wholesale, discuss this with your council EHO during registration and consider whether a commercial shared kitchen is more appropriate for your scale.
Does my home food business need a food safety program?
Category 2 home-based businesses are not required by law to have a full HACCP-based food safety program, but your local council may request one as part of the registration process. We strongly recommend developing a simplified food safety program anyway — it helps you manage risks, trains your staff (even if that is just you), and demonstrates proactive compliance during inspection. Contact us if you need a home-business-tailored food safety program.
Can I use my home oven for both family meals and commercial production?
Yes, but you must manage the cross-contamination risk. Schedule commercial production at a different time from family cooking, clean and sanitise all surfaces and equipment before and after commercial use, and store commercial ingredients and packaging separately from household food. Many home-based businesses use colour-coded containers and labels to distinguish commercial stock from family food — this level of organisation impresses EHOs during inspection.
