Cleaning and Sanitation Schedule Guide for Food Businesses

A properly documented cleaning and sanitation schedule is a legal requirement under Standard 3.2.2 — Food Safety Practices and General Requirements and Standard 3.2.2A — Food Safety Management Tools of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code. Every NSW food business must demonstrate that its premises, fixtures, fittings, and equipment are maintained in a clean and sanitary condition at all times.

The NSW Food Authority routinely inspects cleaning records during council visits. A missing or inadequate cleaning schedule is one of the most common non-conformances identified in food premises across the state. This guide explains how to build a compliant cleaning schedule, what areas to include, chemical safety requirements, and verification methods.

Why a Cleaning Schedule Matters

A cleaning schedule is more than a list of chores. It is a documented food safety control that ensures:

  • Every area of your premises is cleaned at the appropriate frequency
  • Cleaning methods are standardised across all staff
  • Chemicals are used safely and correctly
  • Cleaning effectiveness is verified and recorded
  • Due diligence can be demonstrated during an audit or inspection

Your food safety program (or HACCP plan) must reference the cleaning schedule as a supporting prerequisite program. If you are developing a new food safety program, our food safety consulting team can integrate cleaning schedules into your overall compliance framework.

What to Include in a Cleaning Schedule

Every cleaning schedule should contain the following elements for each task:

Item or Area to Be Cleaned

List every surface, piece of equipment, and area in your premises. This includes floors, walls, ceilings, benchtops, sinks, exhaust canopies, ovens, fryers, cool rooms, freezers, shelving, cutting boards, utensils, dishwashers, handwash stations, waste bins, and staff amenities.

Cleaning Method

Specify the step-by-step procedure. For example: “Pre-rinse with warm water, apply detergent solution with a colour-coded cloth (blue for food contact surfaces), scrub, rinse, apply sanitiser (200 ppm quaternary ammonium compound, 2-minute contact time), air dry.”

Chemicals and Concentrations

List the specific cleaning and sanitising chemicals to be used, including the required dilution rates (e.g., 5 mL per litre of water) and contact times. All chemicals must be approved for use in food premises and have current Safety Data Sheets (SDS) on site.

Frequency

Define how often each task must be completed. Common frequencies include:

  • After each use: Cutting boards, knives, food preparation equipment
  • Daily: Floors, benchtops, sinks, handwash stations
  • Weekly: Cool room shelves, ovens, exhaust canopies
  • Monthly: Walls, ceilings, storerooms, waste areas
  • Quarterly or annually: Deep cleaning of ductwork, grease traps, ventilation systems

Responsible Person

Assign each task to a specific team member or role (e.g., “Morning shift chef” or “Cleaner — John Smith”). This creates accountability.

Verification and Sign-Off

Each completed task must be signed off by the person who performed it and verified by a supervisor or FSS. Verification methods include visual inspection, ATP swabbing (for high-risk environments), and allergen swab testing.

Chemical Safety Requirements

Under Standard 3.2.2, Division 5, all chemicals used in a food premises must be:

  • Approved for use in food-handling areas
  • Stored in clearly labelled containers away from food and food-contact surfaces
  • Accompanied by a current Safety Data Sheet (SDS) readily accessible to staff
  • Used strictly according to manufacturer instructions, including dilution rates and contact times

Never store chemicals above food preparation areas or in the same cupboard as dry goods. Use a dedicated chemical storage cabinet or a clearly segregated lower shelf. All staff must be trained in the correct use of each chemical. Our food safety training includes a module on chemical safety and cleaning procedures.

Colour-Coded Cleaning Equipment

The NSW Food Authority recommends colour-coded cleaning equipment to prevent cross-contamination. The industry-standard system is:

  • Red: Toilets and bathroom areas
  • Yellow: General surfaces (walls, floors, windows)
  • Blue: Food contact surfaces (benchtops, cutting boards)
  • Green: Kitchen and food preparation areas
  • White: High-risk or allergen-control zones

Your cleaning schedule should reference which colour equipment is allocated to each area.

Cleaning Schedule Verification

Verification proves that cleaning has been effective. Methods include:

  • Visual inspection: Check for visible soil, grease, or residue
  • Touch inspection: Surfaces should feel clean and dry
  • Smell inspection: No lingering chemical or foul odours
  • ATP bioluminescence testing: Measures organic residue on surfaces (recommended for high-risk businesses)
  • Microbiological swabbing: Laboratory testing for specific pathogens (for manufacturers)

Verification results must be recorded and reviewed by the FSS. Our internal auditing services include cleaning effectiveness checks as part of every site audit.

Record Retention

Completed cleaning schedules must be retained for a minimum of 12 months. For businesses in high-risk sectors (e.g., ready-to-eat food manufacturing, aged care, hospitals), we recommend retaining records for at least 2 years. Inspecting councils and the NSW Food Authority routinely request cleaning records going back at least 3–6 months.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should commercial kitchen walls and ceilings be cleaned?

Walls and ceilings should be cleaned at least monthly in a standard food premises. In high-risk environments (e.g., hospitals, aged care), a weekly or fortnightly schedule is recommended. Grease extractor canopies should be professionally cleaned at least quarterly, or more frequently depending on cooking volume.

Can I use bleach (sodium hypochlorite) as a food contact surface sanitiser?

Yes, but only at the correct concentration. For food contact surfaces, use 100 ppm (parts per million) available chlorine with a 30-second contact time. Bleach must be rinsed off with potable water after contact unless the label specifies no-rinse. All chemical use must follow the manufacturer's instructions and be documented in your cleaning schedule.

What is the difference between cleaning and sanitising?

Cleaning removes visible soil, grease, and food debris using detergent and mechanical action. Sanitising reduces the number of microorganisms on a surface to a safe level using heat or chemical sanitiser. Both steps are essential. You must clean before you sanitise, because sanitisers cannot penetrate through organic soil.
