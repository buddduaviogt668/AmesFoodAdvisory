Corrective Action Procedures

When things go wrong in your food business, a robust corrective action procedure protects your customers and your compliance. This guide covers when corrective action is needed, root cause analysis, documentation, and verification.

Why Corrective Action Procedures Matter for NSW Food Businesses

Under the Food Standards Code Standard 3.2.2 (Clause 12), every NSW food business must have a system for correcting problems when food safety goes off-track. This is not optional — it is a legal requirement. A corrective action procedure (CAP) is the mechanism by which you identify, document, fix, and prevent recurrence of food safety failures. Whether it is a fridge that drifted above 5°C, a batch of undercooked chicken, or a cleaning chemical found near open food, your response must be systematic, documented, and effective.

A well-designed corrective action procedure does more than keep you compliant. It protects your customers from harm, reduces waste from reprocessing or disposal, builds a culture of continuous improvement, and demonstrates due diligence to auditors and regulators. If you do not yet have a CAP framework in place, our HACCP plan development service can help you build one from the ground up.

When Is Corrective Action Required?

Corrective action is triggered whenever a critical limit is breached or a food safety deviation occurs. Common scenarios include:

  • Temperature abuse: A refrigerated cool room is recorded at 8°C for an unknown period. Potentially hazardous foods may need to be condemned or diverted.
  • Failed cooking temperature: A batch of chicken breasts reaches only 68°C instead of the required 75°C. Immediate corrective action is needed.
  • Allergen cross-contact: A customer receives a meal containing their declared allergen due to a recipe mix-up or equipment contamination.
  • Cleaning failure: Routine environmental swabs return positive results for Listeria or E. coli in a ready-to-eat area.
  • Equipment malfunction: A dishwashing machine fails to reach sanitising temperature (above 77°C for chemical sanitisation, or above 80°C for heat sanitisation).
  • Pest activity: Rodent droppings are found in the dry goods storage area.

In each of these cases, your Food Safety Program should define the corrective action steps in advance. Staff must be trained to recognise a deviation and initiate the procedure immediately. Our SOP writing service can help you document clear, actionable corrective action workflows for your team.

The Four-Step Corrective Action Process

Step 1: Identify and Isolate

As soon as a deviation is detected, the affected product or process must be identified and isolated. Place a "DO NOT USE" hold on all affected food items. If the issue involves a process (e.g., a dishwashing machine), stop using that equipment until it is verified as safe. Record the time, date, product details, and batch/lot numbers where applicable.

Step 2: Evaluate the Risk

Determine whether the affected food is safe for consumption. This may involve:

  • Checking temperature records to assess time-temperature exposure.
  • Visual inspection for signs of spoilage or contamination.
  • Microbiological testing if available (environmental swabs or product samples).
  • Consulting your Food Safety Program or contacting an expert.

Based on the evaluation, you will decide to: (a) release the product as safe, (b) reprocess it (e.g., re-cook to safe temperature), (c) divert it to a lower-risk use, or (d) condemn and dispose of it.

Step 3: Immediate Corrective Action

Take action to bring the process back under control. Examples include:

  • Adjusting thermostat settings and calling a refrigeration technician.
  • Re-cooking the product to the required core temperature.
  • Re-cleaning and sanitising the affected area or equipment.
  • Retraining the staff member involved in the deviation.

The immediate corrective action must be documented on a Corrective Action Report (CAR). Include what the deviation was, when it was detected, what immediate action was taken, and who authorised it.

Step 4: Root Cause Analysis and Preventive Action

This is the most critical step — and the one most food businesses skip. Without identifying the root cause, the same problem will recur. Use one of these proven root cause analysis techniques:

  • 5 Whys: Ask "why" five times to drill down from symptom to root cause. For example: Why did the fridge temp rise? → The door was left open. → Why was it left open? → Staff were loading deliveries. → Why wasn't the door closed between loads? → No procedure for delivery handling. → Root cause: lack of delivery procedure and training.
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram: Map potential causes across categories — People, Process, Equipment, Materials, Environment, Measurement.
  • Cause-and-Effect Analysis: For complex failures, trace the sequence of events leading to the deviation.

Once the root cause is identified, implement a preventive action to stop it happening again. Examples include: updating procedures, installing door alarms on cool rooms, increasing staff training frequency, or changing supplier specifications. The entire analysis must be documented and reviewed at your next management review meeting.

Documenting Corrective Actions: What Must Be Recorded

Your corrective action records must be detailed enough to withstand scrutiny from the NSW Food Authority or a third-party auditor. At minimum, each CAR should include:

  • Date and time of the deviation.
  • Description of the deviation and product(s) affected.
  • Immediate corrective action taken and person responsible.
  • Disposition of affected product (released, reprocessed, diverted, condemned).
  • Root cause analysis findings.
  • Preventive action implemented.
  • Verification that the corrective action was effective.
  • Sign-off by the Food Safety Supervisor or Manager.

All CARs should be logged in a central register and reviewed regularly for trends. If you are seeing the same type of deviation repeatedly, that is a sign your preventive actions are not working. Verification: Closing the Loop

Corrective action is not complete until you verify that the fix worked. Verification methods include:

  • Temperature monitoring: After a fridge repair, monitor and record temperatures for 48 hours to confirm stability.
  • Visual inspection: After re-cleaning, inspect the area and take ATP swabs if available.
  • Audit of records: Review CARs to ensure all fields are completed and signed off.
  • Staff observation: Observe the staff member who was retrained to confirm they are following the correct procedure.

Verification records must be kept as part of your food safety documentation. Without them, an auditor will consider the corrective action incomplete.

Corrective Action and NSW Regulatory Requirements

The NSW Food Authority expects corrective action procedures to be proportionate to the risk. For a small café, this might be a simple logbook. For a large manufacturer, it should be a formal CAPA (Corrective Action Preventive Action) system integrated with your Quality Management System. Key NSW-specific requirements include:

  • Your Food Safety Program must outline corrective actions for each Critical Control Point (CCP).
  • Food Safety Supervisors (mandatory for most NSW businesses) must be trained to identify deviations and initiate corrective actions.
  • Records must be kept for at least 6 months (for most businesses) or longer if specified by your local council or certification body.
  • In the event of a food recall, corrective action records must be produced to the NSW Food Authority within 24 hours of request.

Building a Culture of Continuous Improvement

The best NSW food businesses treat corrective actions not as punishment, but as learning opportunities. When staff feel safe reporting near-misses without blame, you capture important data that helps prevent serious incidents. Tips for building this culture include:

  • Review CARs openly at team meetings (without naming individuals).
  • Celebrate when a root cause analysis leads to a meaningful improvement.
  • Keep the CAR process simple — if it takes 30 minutes of paperwork for a minor deviation, staff will avoid reporting.
  • Use technology where possible: digital CAR systems with automated reminders reduce the burden.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

How long do I need to keep corrective action records in NSW?
Under Standard 3.2.2, records relating to the Food Safety Program (including corrective actions) must be kept for at least 6 months from the date they were made. However, many certification bodies and local councils require a minimum of 12 months. For high-risk products with long shelf lives, we recommend keeping records for the shelf life of the product plus 6 months. Check with your local council or certification body for specific requirements.
What is the difference between corrective action and preventive action?
Corrective action addresses an existing deviation — cooling a fridge that is too warm, for example. Preventive action addresses the root cause to stop it happening again — installing a door alarm on the cool room. A complete CAPA (Corrective Action Preventive Action) system requires both. Many NSW businesses document corrective actions but neglect the preventive step, which is a common audit finding.
Can I use a template for my corrective action reports?
Yes, and we recommend it. A standardised CAR form ensures consistency and completeness. Your template should include all the fields listed in the documentation section above.
