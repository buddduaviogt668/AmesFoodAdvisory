Food Safety for Childcare Centres in NSW: A Complete Compliance Guide

Childcare centres in New South Wales face a critical responsibility: providing safe, nutritious food to children under five whose immune systems are still developing. Young children are disproportionately affected by foodborne illness — they become dehydrated faster, their stomach acid is less effective at killing pathogens, and they have limited ability to communicate symptoms. A single food safety lapse in a childcare setting can affect dozens of children and lead to serious public health consequences.

This guide covers the specific food safety obligations for childcare centres under the Food Act 2003 (NSW), Food Regulation 2015 (NSW), and Food Standards Code, with a focus on food safety programs, Food Safety Supervisor (FSS) requirements, special dietary needs, and practical strategies for compliance in the early childhood education and care (ECEC) environment.

Regulatory requirements for childcare food services

Most childcare centres that prepare and serve food in NSW are classified as Category 2 food businesses under the Food Regulation 2015 — unless they serve potentially hazardous food to vulnerable children with specific medical conditions, in which case they may be assessed as Category 1. Regardless of classification, all centres must be registered or notified with their local council and comply with Standard 3.2.2 (Food Safety Practices and General Requirements) and Standard 3.2.3 (Food Premises and Equipment) of the Food Standards Code.

In addition to food safety regulations, childcare centres must meet the National Quality Framework (NQF) and Education and Care Services National Regulations, which include specific requirements around food and drink provision, dietary requirements, and allergy management. This dual regulatory layer means your food safety program must align with both the NSW food regulatory system and the ECEC quality standards.

Centre directors should confirm their classification with their local council EHO and ensure their food safety compliance framework addresses both sets of obligations.

Developing a childcare-specific food safety program

While the HACCP principles are universal, a food safety program for a childcare centre must account for the specific hazards that arise in the ECEC context. These include:

  • Large volumes of food prepared in advance and held for staggered meal times across multiple rooms
  • Frequent handling of food by educators (not trained cooks) during family-style dining
  • High-risk menu items such as milk, yoghurt, cooked rice, cut fruit, and egg-based dishes
  • Presence of multiple children with diagnosed food allergies requiring strict separation
  • Bottle preparation and storage for infants under 12 months

Your food safety program should document every food handling step — from order receipt through storage, preparation, cooking, cooling, holding, serving, and waste disposal. Include specific time-temperature parameters for each high-risk food item. If you use a rotational menu (common in ECEC), ensure the hazard analysis covers every menu iteration.

Food Safety Supervisor requirements for childcare centres

Under the Food Regulation 2015 (NSW), all food businesses in NSW must appoint a Food Safety Supervisor (FSS). For childcare centres, the required unit of competency is SITXFSA005 (Use hygienic practices for food safety) and SITXFSA006 (Participate in safe food handling practices) — or the equivalent health-focused unit HLTFSE005. The FSS must be reasonably available at all times the centre is operating, including during school holiday periods if the centre remains open.

In practice, this means having at least one FSS-trained educator on site during every operating hour. Many centres choose to train their cook, centre director, and a senior educator to ensure coverage. The FSS is responsible for: overseeing daily temperature checks, verifying cleaning and sanitising schedules, managing allergen controls, maintaining training records, and responding to any food safety incidents.

Special dietary needs and allergy management

Childcare centres regularly manage children with diagnosed food allergies, intolerances, and cultural or religious dietary requirements. Under the Food Standards Code, the nine declared allergens must be declared and controlled. In a childcare setting, the risk of cross-contact is heightened because multiple children eat in the same room, from shared tables, with educators moving between children.

A robust allergy management system includes:

  • An individualised dietary management plan for each child with a diagnosed allergy, signed by the parent and reviewed by a medical professional
  • A visible allergen matrix in the kitchen and in each room, listing every menu item and its allergen status
  • Colour-coded utensils, cutting boards, and serving dishes for allergen-free meals
  • Seating arrangements that separate children with severe allergies at meal times where practical
  • Designated allergen-free preparation areas in the kitchen
  • Educator training on recognising anaphylaxis symptoms and administering adrenaline auto-injectors

Document every element in your SOP for allergen management. During a council audit, the EHO will ask to see your allergy management plans and evidence of staff training.

Bottle preparation and infant formula safety

For centres caring for children under 12 months, bottle preparation is a high-risk activity requiring strict protocols. Powdered infant formula is not sterile and can contain Cronobacter sakazakii or Salmonella. The NSW Food Authority recommends that formula be prepared using water heated to at least 70°C to kill any pathogens, then cooled to feeding temperature.

Specific requirements for bottle preparation:

  • Designate a clean, separate area for bottle preparation away from food prep and nappy change areas
  • Use a kettle or water boiler that can heat water to 70°C — not a microwave
  • Prepare bottles fresh for each feed; do not store prepared bottles for later use unless refrigerated immediately and used within 24 hours
  • Label each bottle with the child's name, date, and time of preparation
  • Discard any unfinished formula within one hour of the feed starting
  • Clean and sterilise bottles and teats after every use — dishwasher sterilisation is acceptable if it reaches 82°C

Temperature control in childcare kitchens

Childcare kitchens typically operate with limited hours — food is prepared in the morning and served across morning tea, lunch, and afternoon tea. This extended holding period requires meticulous temperature management. Standard 3.2.2 requires that potentially hazardous food be kept at or below 5°C or at or above 60°C.

Practical temperature control tips for childcare:

  • Cook food in small batches to minimise the time it spends in the temperature danger zone
  • Cool cooked food within two hours from 60°C to 21°C and within a further four hours to 5°C — use shallow pans and a blast chiller if available
  • Reheat food to 60°C within two hours and serve immediately; do not reheat food more than once
  • Record temperatures at receiving, cooking, cooling, holding, and service in a bound logbook
  • Calibrate thermometers weekly using the ice-point method (0°C) and document results

Cleaning and sanitising in a childcare kitchen

The frequency of cleaning in a childcare kitchen must be higher than in most hospitality settings because of the volume of meals prepared and the vulnerability of the clientele. Your cleaning schedule should specify tasks to be completed after every meal service, at the end of each day, and weekly.

High-touch and high-risk areas include:

  • Food-contact surfaces (benches, cutting boards, utensils) — clean and sanitise between each menu item
  • Refrigerator handles and door seals — clean daily
  • Highchairs and tables in dining areas — clean and sanitise after every meal
  • Sinks and taps — clean daily
  • Waste bins — empty and clean daily

Use food-grade sanitiser at the correct concentration (typically 50–200 ppm chlorine or quaternary ammonium compounds at label rate). Test sanitiser strength with test strips and document results.

Pest control in childcare centres

Pest control is a particular challenge in childcare centres because of the constant presence of food, children's eating areas, and the proximity of nappy-changing facilities. NSW regulations require that food premises be maintained in a condition that prevents the harbourage of pests. Engage a licensed pest controller to conduct inspections at least quarterly, and keep all pest control records available for audit.

Key pest prevention measures include:

  • Sealing gaps around pipes and walls in the kitchen
  • Fitting flyscreens to windows and doors that open
  • Storing all food in sealed pest-proof containers
  • Removing rubbish daily and keeping bins lidded and away from the building
  • Inspect delivered goods for signs of pest activity before accepting them

Audit preparation for childcare centres

Your local council EHO may inspect your centre annually or biennially. The inspection will focus on:

  • The food safety program and its implementation
  • Temperature records — ideally the previous 12 months
  • FSS certification and staff training records
  • Cleaning schedules and chemical storage
  • Pest control records
  • Allergen management documentation

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Does my childcare centre need a food safety program in NSW?
Yes, if you serve potentially hazardous food such as cooked meat, dairy products, eggs, rice, or cut fruit. Most childcare centres that prepare meals on-site need a documented food safety program based on HACCP principles. If you only provide pre-packaged, shelf-stable foods brought from home, you may not need a full program, but you must still meet the general food safety requirements of the Food Standards Code and maintain appropriate hygiene practices.
How many Food Safety Supervisors does a childcare centre need?
NSW law requires at least one FSS who is reasonably available at all times the centre operates. In practice, we recommend training two to three staff members — typically the cook, the centre director, and a senior educator — to ensure coverage across shifts, holidays, and staff absences. The FSS must hold a current Statement of Attainment in the relevant unit of competency (SITXFSA005/SITXFSA006 or HLTFSE005).
Can we serve food brought from home in a childcare centre?
Yes, but you must manage the food safety risks. Home-prepared food is not subject to the same controls as commercial food. Centres should have a policy that specifies: parents must provide food in labelled, sealed containers; food must be transported in an insulated cooler with an ice brick; high-risk foods such as dairy, egg, and meat dishes should be avoided in lunchboxes; and educators must check the temperature of stored lunchbox items before serving. Uneaten home-provided food must be discarded at the end of the day — it must not be returned home or re-served.
