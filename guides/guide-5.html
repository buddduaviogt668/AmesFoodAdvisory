Allergen Management Plan Guide for NSW: Compliance Under Standard 3.2.2A

Food allergies are not a trend. They are a serious, potentially life-threatening medical condition affecting approximately 1 in 10 Australian infants, 1 in 20 children, and 1 in 50 adults. For food businesses in NSW, managing allergens is no longer just good practice; it is the law.

With the full enforcement of Standard 3.2.2A of the FSANZ Food Standards Code, every food business that handles high-risk food in NSW must have a documented allergen management plan. This guide covers exactly what your plan needs to include, how to prevent cross-contamination, and how to train your team to handle customer allergen enquiries confidently.

The Nine Declared Allergens Under Australian Law

In Australia, the Food Standards Code requires the declaration of nine allergens on food labels and in food service settings:

  • Peanuts
  • Tree nuts (almonds, cashews, walnuts, pecans, pistachios, macadamias, Brazil nuts, hazelnuts, pine nuts, chestnuts)
  • Milk and milk products (lactose included)
  • Eggs
  • Wheat and other cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats, spelt, triticale)
  • Soybeans and soy products
  • Fish and fish products
  • Crustaceans and crustacean products (prawns, lobster, crab)
  • Sesame seeds and sesame seed products

Lupin and sulphites (added at more than 10 mg/kg) are also required to be declared in certain contexts. Unlike the EU or the US, Australia does not currently mandate the declaration of mustard or celery, but that may change as the Code evolves.

Under Standard 1.2.3 of the Code, these allergens must be declared on packaged food labels. In food service settings (restaurants, cafes, takeaway), customers have the right to ask about allergens, and your business must be able to provide accurate information. Our consulting services can help you set up systems to manage allergen enquiries efficiently.

What Standard 3.2.2A Requires for Allergen Management

Standard 3.2.2A was introduced in December 2023 and is now fully enforced across NSW. For allergen management, it requires:

  • A documented allergen management procedure as part of your food safety program
  • Evidence that food handlers have been trained on allergen risks and cross-contact prevention
  • Clear processes for communicating allergen information to customers

Your allergen management plan should be a dedicated section within your HACCP-based food safety program (see our HACCP guide). If your existing program does not have a standalone allergen section, it is now non-compliant. We cover this gap regularly in our internal audits.

Building Your Allergen Management Plan: Step by Step

A robust allergen management plan does not need to be complicated, but it does need to be thorough. Here is the structure we recommend:

Step 1: Ingredient Inventory and Supplier Communication
Create a complete inventory of every ingredient you use in your business. For each ingredient, identify which of the nine declared allergens it contains (or may contain). Work with your suppliers to obtain accurate allergen information, including any changes to product formulations. This is why supplier approval procedures (covered in your food safety program) are so important.

Step 2: Recipe and Menu Review
Map every menu item against the ingredient inventory to identify which allergens are present. This sounds simple, but it becomes complex when you consider that recipes change, suppliers change ingredients, and staff may substitute items without thinking about allergens. A living allergen matrix (updated whenever a recipe changes) is essential.

Step 3: Cross-Contact Prevention
Cross-contact (also called cross-contamination) happens when an allergen is unintentionally transferred from one food to another. In a busy NSW kitchen, this can happen in many ways:

  • Using the same chopping board for a nut-containing dessert and a nut-free meal
  • Frying gluten-free fish in oil that was used to cook crumbed chicken
  • Storing raw prawns above ready-to-eat salads
  • Using the same tongs for multiple dishes
  • Sharing equipment like toasters, mixers, or blenders without thorough cleaning

Your allergen management plan must identify every point where cross-contact could occur and specify the control measures in place. This might include dedicated equipment, colour-coded utensils, segregated storage, and thorough cleaning procedures between runs.

Step 4: Customer Communication
Under the Food Standards Code, food businesses must provide allergen information to customers upon request. This is not optional. Your plan should include:

  • A process for recording allergen requests at the point of ordering
  • Staff training on how to respond to allergen questions (never guess; check the recipe matrix or ask the chef)
  • A clear statement on your menu that allergen information is available upon request
  • Processes for handling special dietary modifications without cross-contact

Step 5: Staff Training

Your allergen management plan is only as good as your team's ability to execute it. Every food handler needs to understand:

  • The nine declared allergens and where they hide
  • The difference between a food allergy, intolerance, and coeliac disease
  • How cross-contact happens and how to prevent it
  • How to read ingredient labels and identify allergen declarations
  • How to respond to customer allergen enquiries
  • What to do if an allergic reaction is suspected (call 000 immediately)

We offer accredited food safety training that includes a dedicated allergen management module, delivered by a TAE-qualified trainer with real experience in NSW food businesses.

Step 6: Verification and Review
Your allergen management plan must be a living document. Verify it regularly by:

  • Conducting internal audits that specifically check allergen controls
  • Reviewing supplier specifications when ingredient formulations change
  • Testing your team with scenario-based questions ("A customer says they have a severe nut allergy and want the pad Thai. What do you do?")
  • Reviewing and updating your allergen matrix whenever your menu changes

Allergen Management for Different Business Types

Cafes and Restaurants
In a typical cafe, the highest-risk areas are the pastry cabinet (nuts, dairy, eggs), the fryer (shared oil), and the sandwich station (cross-contact from utensils). Simple solutions include segregated preparation areas, dedicated tongs for allergen-free items, and a clear system for marking modified orders.

Bakeries
Bakeries face unique challenges because wheat flour is everywhere. Cross-contact between gluten-free and regular products is a constant risk. Dedicated production runs, thorough cleaning between batches, and separate storage for gluten-free ingredients are critical.

Manufacturers
Manufacturers require more sophisticated controls, including supplier auditing, cleaning validation, and rework management. If you use shared production lines, you need a documented changeover procedure and allergen swab testing to verify cleaning effectiveness.

Takeaway and Fast Food
High-volume, fast-paced environments are prone to cross-contact through shared utensils, fryer oil, and rushed preparation. Clear recipe cards, pre-portioned ingredients, and customer order annotation systems are essential.

The "May Contain" Trap

One common mistake we see is the overuse of precautionary allergen labelling ("may contain traces of nuts"). While this might seem like a safe legal move, the NSW Food Authority has made it clear that precautionary labels should only be used when there is a genuine, documented risk of cross-contact that cannot be controlled. Using "may contain" as a blanket disclaimer is considered poor practice and can be misleading to allergic consumers.

A better approach is to implement controls that minimise cross-contact, then label honestly based on what your controls can achieve. Our consultants can help you assess your cross-contact risks and determine appropriate labelling.

The Legal Consequences of Getting It Wrong

An allergic reaction linked to your business can have devastating consequences, both for the customer and for you. Under the Food Act 2003 (NSW), selling food that causes an allergic reaction due to undeclared allergens is a serious offence. Penalties include:

  • On-the-spot fines for inadequate allergen labelling or failure to provide allergen information
  • Prosecution and court-imposed penalties if the failure results in injury
  • Civil liability claims for damages
  • Reputational damage that can destroy a small business

Beyond the legal consequences, there is a human one. Food allergies can be fatal. Your allergen management plan is not just a compliance document; it is a life-saving tool.

Putting Your Allergen Plan into Action

Here is a quick action checklist to get your allergen management plan off the ground:

  • Create your ingredient inventory and allergen matrix
  • Identify cross-contact risks in your kitchen and implement controls
  • Write your allergen management procedures and add them to your food safety program
  • Train your team and document the training
  • Set up a customer allergen enquiry log
  • Conduct a trial run where you test your team's knowledge
  • Schedule a quarterly review of your plan

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to list allergens verbally or in writing when a customer asks?
You can provide allergen information verbally, but it is strongly recommended that you have a written allergen matrix available to ensure accuracy. Many NSW councils expect to see a documented system for managing allergen enquiries, and verbal-only processes are difficult to verify during an inspection. A written matrix also protects your staff from relying on memory under pressure.
Does Standard 3.2.2A apply to bakeries and cake shops?
Yes. Bakeries and cake shops handle high-risk foods (eggs, dairy, sometimes meat or seafood) and are required to comply with all three food safety management tools under Standard 3.2.2A, including a documented allergen management plan. Given that bakeries commonly handle wheat, nuts, eggs, and dairy, allergen cross-contact is a particularly significant risk in this sector.
What is the difference between a food allergy and an intolerance?
A food allergy involves the immune system and can cause severe, life-threatening reactions (anaphylaxis) even from trace amounts. An intolerance (such as lactose intolerance) involves the digestive system and typically causes discomfort but is not life-threatening. Your team needs to understand this difference because the level of risk and the required controls are vastly different. A customer with coeliac disease, for example, cannot tolerate even trace amounts of gluten, and the controls needed are similar to those for a severe allergy.
