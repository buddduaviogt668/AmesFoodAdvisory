Food Safety Program Template Guide for NSW: What Must Be in Your Program Under Standard 3.2.1

If you own or manage a food business in NSW, you are legally required to have a food safety program. This is not optional, and it is not something you can download from the internet and sign your name to. Under Standard 3.2.1 of the FSANZ Food Standards Code, your food safety program must be based on HACCP principles, specific to your business, and implemented every single day.

But what exactly does a compliant food safety program look like? How detailed does it need to be? And what do councils and the NSW Food Authority actually expect to see when they open that binder? This guide answers those questions and provides a clear template structure you can follow.

What Is a Food Safety Program?

A food safety program is a written document that identifies and controls food safety hazards in your specific business. It translates the general requirements of the Food Standards Code into practical procedures that your team can follow.

Think of it as your business's food safety manual. It covers everything from how you receive deliveries to how you serve food, how you clean, how you train staff, and how you handle problems. Under Standard 3.2.1, it must be:

  • Written and accessible to all food handlers
  • Based on HACCP principles (see our HACCP guide for the seven principles)
  • Specific to your business, menu, equipment, and processes
  • Reviewed and updated at least annually, or whenever you change a significant process
  • Signed by the business owner or nominated Food Safety Supervisor

The difference between a compliant program and a generic template is the difference between passing an inspection and receiving a non-compliance notice. We have seen both, many times. The Required Structure of a Compliant Food Safety Program

While there is no single mandatory layout, every food safety program must contain certain elements to meet Standard 3.2.1. Based on what councils and the NSW Food Authority expect, here is the structure we recommend:

1. Business Details and Scope
Start with your business name, address, ABN, contact details, and the scope of your food handling activities. Be specific. "We operate a Thai restaurant serving dine-in, takeaway, and delivery. Menu includes curries, stir-fries, rice dishes, and desserts."

2. Management Commitment and Responsibilities
A signed statement from the business owner confirming commitment to food safety. Include the name and contact details of your nominated Food Safety Supervisor and their responsibilities.

3. Food Process Flow Diagram
A visual map of how food moves through your business, from receiving to serving. Each step should be clearly labelled. The flow diagram is the backbone of your hazard analysis.

4. Hazard Analysis and CCP Identification
For each step in your flow diagram, identify the potential hazards (biological, chemical, physical, and now allergen cross-contact under Standard 3.2.2A). Determine which steps are Critical Control Points and set your critical limits. This section is the core of your HACCP-based program.

5. Monitoring Procedures and Corrective Actions
For each CCP, document:

  • What you monitor (e.g., cooking temperature)
  • How you monitor (e.g., probe thermometer)
  • How often you monitor (e.g., every batch during service)
  • Who monitors (e.g., the head chef)
  • The critical limit (e.g., 74°C for 2 minutes)
  • What corrective action to take if the limit is breached (e.g., continue cooking until temperature is reached, or discard and record)

6. Cleaning and Sanitation Schedule
A detailed schedule covering every area of your premises. Include what to clean, how to clean it, what chemical to use, at what concentration, how often, and who is responsible. The NSW Food Authority expects to see cleaning logs completed and signed daily.

7. Pest Control Procedures
Document your pest control arrangements. Include your pest control contractor details, service frequency, bait station map, and procedures for responding to pest sightings.

8. Supplier and Receiving Controls
Approved supplier list, receiving inspection criteria (what you check when deliveries arrive), and procedures for rejecting non-compliant goods. Temperature checks on receipt are critical.

9. Allergen Management Procedures
With Standard 3.2.2A now in effect, a standalone allergen management section is essential. Our Allergen Management Plan Guide has the full details, but your food safety program should at minimum cover your 9 declared allergens, how you prevent cross-contact, and how you handle customer allergen enquiries.

10. Training and Competency
Document your training procedures. Include how you onboard new staff, ongoing food safety training, and how you verify competency. Attach copies of training records and FSS certificates.

11. Record-Keeping Register
List all the records your business must keep and their retention periods. Under the Food Act 2003, most records should be kept for at least 12 months. Key records include:

  • Temperature monitoring logs
  • Cleaning and sanitation logs
  • Corrective action reports
  • Training records
  • Pest control service reports
  • Calibration records for thermometers
  • Customer complaint register

12. Review and Continuous Improvement
A procedure for reviewing and updating your food safety program. Include a section for documenting changes and a register of who has read and understood the current version.

Common Food Safety Program Mistakes to Avoid

We review food safety programs regularly as part of our internal auditing service. Here are the most common issues we find:

  • Using a one-size-fits-all template. If your program mentions a deli slicer and you run a pizza shop, the EHO will know it is not your program.
  • No flow diagram. This is a critical requirement under HACCP Principle 2. Every inspection we know of includes a request for the flow diagram.
  • Missing critical limits. It is not enough to say "cook thoroughly." You need specific, measurable limits (time and temperature).
  • No blank forms. Your program is useless if it does not include the actual templates your team will use for temperature logs, cleaning schedules, and corrective actions.
  • No allergen section. Pre-Standard 3.2.2A, this was a common gap. Now, it is a compliance requirement.
  • Outdated contact details. If your Food Safety Supervisor has left the business and the program still lists them, that is a non-compliance.

How to Make Your Food Safety Program Work in Real Life

A binder on a shelf that nobody reads is not a food safety program; it is a liability. A real program is a working document that your team uses every day. Here is how to make that happen:

  • Keep it accessible. Place laminated quick-reference sheets near work stations (temperature limits, cleaning procedures, corrective actions).
  • Train your team. Every person who handles food needs to know where the program is, what their responsibilities are, and how to complete the required records.
  • Assign ownership. Your Food Safety Supervisor should be responsible for maintaining the program and ensuring it stays current.
  • Review regularly. Set a calendar reminder for your annual review. Also update the program whenever you change your menu, equipment, or layout.
  • Use your corrective action records. When something goes wrong, document it. A record of a corrected issue is evidence of a functioning system, not a failure.

What to Do If You Already Have a Program

If you have an existing food safety program, it is worth conducting a gap analysis. The introduction of Standard 3.2.2A means many older programs are now non-compliant because they lack:

  • An allergen management section
  • Evidence of food handler training / FSS certification
  • A documented food safety management system (not just policies, but actual implementation records)

Frequently asked questions

Does my food safety program need to be approved by the council?
For most retail and food service businesses, your program does not need pre-approval. However, your council EHO will review it during your next inspection and may require changes if it is inadequate. Certain high-risk businesses (manufacturers, importers, wholesalers) regulated by the NSW Food Authority may need their program reviewed as part of the registration process.
How often should I update my food safety program?
At least once per year. In addition, update your program whenever you change your menu significantly, install new equipment, renovate your kitchen, change your Food Safety Supervisor, or receive a non-compliance notice related to your program. A dated program that has not been reviewed in three years is a red flag during any inspection.
Can I write my own food safety program, or do I need a consultant?
You can write your own program if you understand HACCP principles and the requirements of Standard 3.2.1. Many small business owners do exactly that. However, we recommend having your program reviewed by a food safety professional at least once. The cost of a consultant is small compared to the cost of a closure order, a fine, or a food poisoning outbreak linked to your business.
