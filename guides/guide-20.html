Food Safety for Breweries and Distilleries in NSW: A Complete Compliance Guide

Craft breweries, distilleries, and beverage producers occupy a unique position in the food safety regulatory landscape in NSW. While alcoholic beverages are regulated by NSW Liquor & Gaming for licensing purposes, the food safety obligations fall under the Food Act 2003 (NSW), Food Regulation 2015 (NSW), and the Food Standards Code. If your brewery serves food, runs a cellar door, or manufactures non-alcoholic beverages alongside beer or spirits, you are classified as a food business and must comply with all relevant food safety requirements.

This guide covers HACCP for beverage production, cleaning and sanitation protocols, allergen management (including gluten and sulphites), and NSW-specific licensing and registration requirements for breweries and distilleries.

Does my brewery or distillery need to be registered as a food business?

Yes, in most cases. Under the Food Regulation 2015 (NSW), a facility that manufactures, packages, stores, or serves beverages for human consumption is a food business. This includes:

  • Breweries that package beer, cider, or seltzer for wholesale or retail sale
  • Distilleries producing gin, whisky, vodka, or other spirits
  • Cellar doors serving tastings, paddles, or food
  • Brewpubs that both manufacture and serve beer on site
  • Facilities producing non-alcoholic beverages (kombucha, soft drinks, carbonated water)

You must register or notify your local council in the LGA where production occurs. Some councils classify breweries as Category 2 (lower risk), while others assess them as Category 1 if they serve potentially hazardous food from a kitchen. If you operate a cellar door serving food, you are effectively a food premises and subject to full food safety requirements, including a food safety program.

HACCP for beverage production

While beer and spirits have natural antimicrobial properties (low pH, alcohol content, low water activity in spirits), HACCP principles still apply throughout the production process. The NSW Food Authority expects beverage producers to have documented food safety controls covering the entire supply chain â€” from raw ingredient receiving to packaged product dispatch.

The critical control points (CCPs) in a typical brewery or distillery include:

  • Raw material receiving: verify malt, hops, yeast, grains, and adjuncts for quality, temperature (if refrigerated), and pest damage
  • Mashing and lautering: time-temperature controls to prevent enzymatic failure and microbial growth in the mash tun
  • Fermentation: temperature control and vessel sanitation to prevent wild yeast or bacterial contamination
  • Filtration and clarification: filter integrity checks, prevention of cross-contamination between batches
  • Carbonation: COâ‚‚ quality verification and dissolved oxygen control
  • Packaging: container cleanliness, seal integrity, date coding, and atmosphere monitoring for canned products
  • Storage and dispatch: temperature control for unpasteurised products, stock rotation (FIFO), and transport cleanliness

Each CCP must have a defined critical limit, monitoring procedure, corrective action, and record-keeping requirement. For example, fermentation temperature for a lager yeast strain might have a critical limit of 10â€“12Â°C. If the temperature exceeds 12Â°C, the corrective action could include: adjusting the cooling jacket, testing for off-flavours, and segregating the batch for assessment.

Cleaning and sanitation protocols

Cleaning in a brewery or distillery is fundamentally different from cleaning in a restaurant kitchen. You are dealing with complex process equipment â€” mash tuns, fermenters, bright tanks, heat exchangers, kegging lines, and bottling/canning machines â€” each requiring a validated Clean-in-Place (CIP) or Clean-out-of-Place (COP) protocol.

A typical CIP cycle includes:

  1. Pre-rinse with water (removes gross soil and residual product)
  2. Caustic wash (1â€“2% sodium hydroxide at 65â€“80Â°C) â€” removes organic soils, proteins, and biofilms
  3. Intermediate rinse with water (removes caustic residue)
  4. Acid wash (0.5â€“1% phosphoric or nitric acid) â€” removes mineral scale and beerstone
  5. Final rinse with water to neutral pH
  6. Sanitisation (peracetic acid, chlorine dioxide, or hot water at 82Â°C+) â€” kills vegetative microorganisms

Each step must be documented in your Standard Operating Procedures, including chemical concentrations, contact times, temperatures, and verification methods (pH strips, ATP swabs, or microbiological testing). Kegging and canning lines require particular attention â€” inadequate cleaning of the filling heads is a common source of post-packaging contamination.

Allergen management: gluten, sulphites, and beyond

Allergen management is a growing compliance focus for beverage producers in NSW. The Food Standards Code requires that the nine declared allergens be declared on labels, and this applies fully to beer, cider, spirits, and non-alcoholic beverages. The key allergens relevant to beverage production are gluten (from barley, wheat, rye, oats) and sulphites (used as a preservative in cider, wine, and some beers).

Specific obligations:

  • If you brew beer with gluten-containing grains, you must declare gluten on the label â€” even if you use clarifying enzymes or gluten-removal processing aids, the product may still contain gluten above the 20 ppm threshold for "gluten-free" claims
  • If you produce cider or wine with added sulphites above 10 mg/L, you must declare "contains sulphites" on the label
  • If you use fining agents (isinglass, gelatine, casein, egg white), these must be assessed for allergen residue â€” casein and egg are declared allergens
  • If you produce gluten-reduced or gluten-free beer, you must validate your testing methodology and retain batch records proving compliance with the 20 ppm standard

Cross-contact risks exist in shared production facilities. If you brew gluten-containing and gluten-free products on the same system, you must validate your cleaning protocol to demonstrate that gluten residue is reduced below 20 ppm between runs. Use ELISA testing to verify, and retain all test results for audit.

NSW licensing requirements for breweries and distilleries

In addition to food business registration, breweries and distilleries in NSW require:

  • Manufacturer's Licence from NSW Liquor & Gaming â€” required to produce alcoholic beverages for sale
  • On-Premises Licence or Cellar Door Licence if you serve tastings or drinks on site
  • Food Business Notification with the local council (as above)
  • Approval from the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) for excise purposes â€” excise is payable on beer and spirits produced in Australia
  • Trade measurement registration if you package products in fixed quantities (bottles, cans, kegs)

NSW Liquor & Gaming may conduct joint inspections with the local council EHO, particularly for venues that serve food. Your food safety program and HACCP documentation should be ready for review during any licensing inspection.

Water quality and testing

Water is the primary ingredient in beer and spirits â€” typically over 90% of the finished product. Under Standard 3.2.3, water used in food production must be potable. For breweries and distilleries, this means:

  • Annual microbiological testing (E. coli, coliforms, total plate count) from the point of use in production
  • Chemical analysis for minerals, heavy metals, and treatment chemicals that could affect product safety or quality
  • If you treat water on site (filtration, reverse osmosis, UV), validate and monitor the treatment process
  • Maintain water test records for at least two years

Traceability and recall readiness

All beverage producers must have a traceability system that can identify the supplier, batch, and customer for every product. Standard 3.2.2 requires that food businesses be able to trace food one step forward (to the customer) and one step back (to the supplier). For breweries, this means maintaining lot-level records for:

  • Raw ingredients (malt lots, hop batches, yeast cultures)
  • Packaging materials (bottles, cans, kegs, lids)
  • Production batches (date, shift, operator, CCP records)
  • Distribution records (wholesaler, retailer, direct customer)

In the event of a contamination incident â€” or the discovery of an undeclared allergen â€” you must be able to identify affected stock and initiate a recall within 24 hours. Maintain a mock recall log and conduct a mock recall drill annually. We can facilitate this through our internal auditing services.

Waste management and environmental compliance

Breweries and distilleries produce significant volumes of organic waste â€” spent grain, hops, yeast slurry, and process water. While these are not typically food safety hazards, they attract pests and create odour issues if not managed correctly. Store spent grain in sealed, covered bins and remove it at least weekly (more frequently in warm weather). Many NSW breweries partner with local farms for spent grain collection â€” ensure your contract specifies collection frequency and that containers are cleaned after each pickup.

Trade waste discharge to sewer requires approval from the local water authority (Sydney Water in Greater Sydney, or your local water utility in regional areas). High-strength organic effluent from breweries can exceed allowable discharge limits for BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) and TSS (total suspended solids). You may need a trade waste agreement and pre-treatment system (pH adjustment, solids screening).

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Food Safety Supervisor for my brewery in NSW?
Yes, if your brewery is registered as a food business â€” which it almost certainly is if you produce beverages for sale. The FSS must hold a Statement of Attainment in the relevant unit (typically SITXFSA005/SITXFSA006 for the hospitality sector or FBPFSY2001 for the food processing sector). The FSS must be reasonably available during production hours. If your brewery also has a kitchen or serves food, you may need an additional FSS with hospitality-sector certification.
Can I label my beer as "gluten-free" if I use gluten-removing enzymes?
Under the Food Standards Code, a product labelled "gluten-free" must contain no detectable gluten (less than 20 ppm). If you use enzymes or processing aids to reduce gluten, you must validate your process with ELISA testing and retain batch records demonstrating compliance below 20 ppm. Many brewers prefer the term "gluten-reduced" or "crafted to remove gluten" unless they have rigorous third-party testing. The NSW Food Authority may request your test results during inspection.
Do I need a food safety program for a distillery that only produces spirits?
If you produce only distilled spirits (which have a very low water activity and high alcohol content), your risk classification may be lower, and a full HACCP-based food safety program may not be legally required â€” but the NSW Food Authority still expects documented food safety controls covering cleaning, allergen management, traceability, and water quality. Most distilleries choose to implement a simplified food safety program anyway, as it provides operational benefits, protects brand reputation, and streamlines council inspections. We can help you develop a scaled program appropriate to your risk profile.
