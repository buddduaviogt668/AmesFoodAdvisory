Food Safety for Catering and Events in NSW: A Complete Compliance Guide

Catering for events in New South Wales adds a layer of complexity that fixed-premise hospitality businesses rarely face. You are preparing food in one kitchen, transporting it across town, holding it in temporary structures, and serving hundreds of guests who trust you to keep them safe. The NSW Food Authority and local councils hold caterers and event food businesses to the same core standards as restaurants, but the operational risks multiply when you introduce mobility, weather, and unfamiliar sites.

Whether you run a mobile food van, a wedding catering company, or a stall at a community festival, understanding the specific food safety obligations tied to temporary food premises, event registration, off-site temperature control, and safe transportation is the difference between a successful event and a public health incident.

This guide walks through every critical requirement under the Food Act 2003 (NSW), Food Standards Code, and NSW Food Authority guidance so you can operate with confidence.

What constitutes a temporary or mobile food premises in NSW?

Under the Food Standards Code, a temporary food premises includes any structure that is not a permanent building — marquees, tents, stalls, pop-up kitchens, mobile vans, and even outdoor cooking stations. The key distinction is that these premises are erected, dismantled, and stored between uses. The NSW Food Authority requires that even temporary setups meet the same fundamental food safety outcomes: cleanable surfaces, protection from contamination, adequate hand-washing facilities, and appropriate temperature control.

If you operate across multiple local government areas (LGAs), you may need to notify each council separately. Some councils in Greater Sydney require a formal Notification of Temporary Food Premises form submitted at least 14 days before the event. It is your responsibility to check the specific requirements of the council where each event is held.

A well-prepared temporary premises checklist should include:

  • A food-grade temporary shelter with side walls to block wind-borne contaminants
  • Food-grade tables and equipment that can be effectively cleaned and sanitised on site
  • An approved hand-washing station with warm running water, soap, and paper towels — not just hand sanitiser
  • An adequate supply of potable water for cleaning and food preparation
  • Waste water containment if no permanent drainage connection is available
  • Temperature monitoring equipment with calibrated probes

Event registration and council notification requirements

In NSW, any business that sells food at a temporary event must be registered or notified with the local council where the event occurs. This applies even if you are already registered with your home council for your primary premises. The Food Regulation 2015 (NSW) requires food businesses operating from temporary premises to ensure their registration covers the event location. Some councils class this as a Category 2 (lower-risk) notification, but depending on the menu — for example, if you handle raw proteins or serve high-risk foods like cooked rice, seafood, or dairy-based sauces — you may be assessed as higher risk and need a full food safety program.

Event organisers often apply for a single "event licence" that covers all vendors, but this does not absolve individual food businesses from meeting their own food safety obligations. At a minimum, you must have a current Food Safety Supervisor (FSS) certificate on display — in NSW, at least one FSS must be on site at all times during operation. The FSS must hold a Statement of Attainment for the relevant food sector (e.g., SITXFSA005 for hospitality).

Practical tip: contact the council's environmental health officer (EHO) at least 21 days before a major event. Provide a site plan showing your layout, a copy of your proposed menu, and your temperature control plan. Proactive engagement reduces the risk of last-minute refusal to operate.

Temperature control at off-site venues: the critical challenge

Temperature abuse is the single most common cause of foodborne illness in catering operations. When you prepare food in a central kitchen and serve it miles away, the window between the 5°C and 60°C danger zone expands dramatically. The Food Standards Code (Standard 3.2.2) requires that potentially hazardous food be kept at or below 5°C or at or above 60°C. For caterers, this means every leg of the journey — from cooking to cooling to transport to holding to service — must be temperature-monitored and documented.

Here is the practical approach: do not rely on a single piece of equipment. Use passive insulation (insulated food-grade containers), active temperature maintenance (hot-holding cabinets, refrigerated vans), and multiple probe thermometers. Assign one staff member the role of "temperature officer" for the event — they log readings at dispatch, arrival, holding, and service. If any reading falls outside the safe zone for more than two hours, the food must be discarded. This is not negotiable under NSW law.

For cold service: pre-chill containers, transport food below 5°C, and use ice beds or gel packs at the serving point. Never add fresh ice to cold food displays — use only food-grade ice manufactured from potable water.

For hot service: pre-heat hot-holding units to at least 65°C before loading. Food must reach 60°C internal temperature within two hours of cooking and stay above 60°C until served. If you cannot guarantee this, reduce the menu to items that can be prepared and served immediately from a cold chain.

Safe food transportation for caterers

Transportation is the gap where most catering food safety plans break down. The physical movement of food creates risks: temperature fluctuation, physical contamination from shifting containers, cross-contamination from raw to ready-to-eat items, and time delays that push food past safe limits. Standard 3.2.2 of the Food Standards Code requires that food transported off-site must be protected from contamination and maintained at the correct temperature.

Your transport plan should address:

  • Separation of raw and cooked foods in sealed, labelled, colour-coded containers
  • Use of refrigerated vehicles or insulated coolers with verified temperature logs
  • Loading sequence that minimises the time food spends outside temperature control
  • Non-slip surfaces and load restraints to prevent container tipping
  • Cleaning and sanitising protocols for transport containers after every use
  • A backup power or ice supply plan in case of vehicle breakdown or delay

Document your transportation procedures in a written Standard Operating Procedure (SOP). This becomes the basis of your defence if an audit or incident investigation occurs.

Waste management and cleaning at temporary sites

Temporary event sites rarely have the permanent drainage and waste infrastructure of a fixed kitchen. NSW regulations require that waste water from hand-washing and cleaning stations be contained and disposed of into a sanitary sewer system — not onto the ground or into stormwater drains. Grey water tanks must be emptied at an approved disposal point after each event.

Solid waste, including used single-use gloves, food scraps, and packaging, must be placed in lidded bins that are secured against animals and insects. At outdoor events in regional NSW, this is especially important due to the prevalence of birds, possums, and flies that can transfer pathogens to food-contact surfaces.

Cleaning chemicals must be food-grade, stored separately from food, and used only when no food preparation is occurring. Sanitising wipes are not a substitute for proper cleaning: all surfaces must be cleaned of visible soil before sanitiser is applied.

Allergen management at events

Catering events carry a high risk for allergen cross-contact because multiple dishes are often prepared in close proximity in a temporary space. Under the Food Standards Code, the nine declared allergens — peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, fish, crustaceans, sesame, soy, and gluten-containing grains — must be declared on menus or at the point of sale. For caterers, this means maintaining an accurate allergen matrix for every dish and training staff on allergen communication.

A best practice is to designate one section of the temporary kitchen as an "allergen-safe" zone where free-from meals are prepared with separate utensils and cutting boards. At the service point, clearly label each dish with its allergen status. Many NSW councils now expect caterers to provide written allergen information on request — verbal communication alone is no longer considered sufficient in an audit context.

Training your event catering team

Even the best documentation is useless if your staff do not follow it. Every person handling food at an event must have adequate skills and knowledge in food safety under Standard 3.2.2, Division 4. For caterers, this means training that specifically covers temperature control, transport protocols, and temporary premises hygiene. A generic online food safety certificate does not prepare staff for the realities of a muddy festival site or a 40°C summer wedding.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a separate food business registration for each event in NSW?
It depends on the council. Many NSW councils accept a mobile food business registration that covers multiple events within their LGA, but you may need to notify them individually of each event date and location. If you operate across multiple council areas, you must register or notify each council separately. We recommend checking with each council's EHO at least 14 days before each event.
What temperature should I transport hot food at for catering?
Hot potentially hazardous food must be kept at or above 60°C during transport. Use insulated hot-holding cabinets that are pre-heated to at least 65°C before loading. Record the internal temperature of the food at the point of dispatch and again on arrival. If the temperature drops below 60°C, you have a two-hour window to reheat it to 60°C or above, or discard it.
Can I use hand sanitiser instead of a hand-washing station at a temporary food stall?
No. The NSW Food Authority requires temporary food premises to have a dedicated hand-washing station with warm running water, soap, and single-use paper towels. Hand sanitiser is not a substitute — it is ineffective against some pathogens (like Norovirus) and does not remove physical soil. Some councils accept a portable hand-washing unit with a water tank, soap dispenser, and waste water collection, but you must still provide warm water.
