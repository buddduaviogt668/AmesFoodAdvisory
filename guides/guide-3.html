How to Pass a Council Food Inspection in NSW: The Complete Preparation Guide

For many food business owners in NSW, the arrival of an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) from the local council triggers a mix of anxiety and dread. But it does not have to. A council food inspection is not a pass-fail exam; it is an assessment of whether your business is meeting its legal obligations under the Food Act 2003 and the FSANZ Food Standards Code. With the right preparation, you can approach inspection day with confidence.

This guide walks you through exactly what inspectors check, how to prepare, and the most common findings that catch businesses off guard. We have conducted hundreds of internal audits and pre-inspection assessments across NSW, and these insights come straight from that experience.

Step 1: Understand What Your Council EHO Is Looking For

Your EHO is not there to trip you up. They are there to verify that your business is operating safely and that you have the systems in place to prove it. Their checklist typically covers five core areas aligned with Standard 3.2.2 and Standard 3.2.3 of the FSANZ Code:

1. Food Safety Program and Documentation
Under Standard 3.2.1, your business must have a documented food safety program based on HACCP principles. The EHO will ask to see it and will check that it is specific to your business, not a generic template you downloaded. They will look for:

  • A current, signed food safety program (see our food safety program template guide)
  • Temperature monitoring logs for fridges, freezers, and hot-hold units
  • Cleaning and sanitation schedules with sign-off records
  • Corrective action reports (have you ever had a temperature breach? What did you do?)
  • Food handler training records including Food Safety Supervisor certificates

2. Temperature Control
Temperature abuse is the number one cause of foodborne illness, and it is the number one thing EHOs check. Under Standard 3.2.2, potentially hazardous food must be stored below 5°C or above 60°C. The EHO will:

  • Check the temperature of your fridges, freezers, and hot-hold units
  • Probe the internal temperature of high-risk foods (cooked chicken, dairy, seafood)
  • Review your temperature monitoring logs for completeness
  • Check that your thermometers are calibrated and that you have a calibration log
  • Observe your cooling and reheating practices

3. Hygiene and Sanitation
The condition of your premises speaks volumes. The EHO will inspect:

  • Hand-washing facilities: are they accessible, stocked with soap and paper towels, and used only for hand washing?
  • Cleaning standards: bench tops, equipment, floors, walls, and ceiling voids
  • Sanitising procedures: are you using the correct concentration of sanitiser? Do you have a test kit?
  • Waste management: bins covered, waste stored away from food, grease traps maintained
  • Pest control: current pest control certificate, insect electrocutors working, no evidence of pests

4. Food Handling Practices
The EHO will observe your team in action. They will look for:

  • Proper hand washing before handling food and after glove changes
  • Avoidance of bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat food
  • Separation of raw and ready-to-eat food during storage and preparation
  • Correct thawing methods (in the cool room, not on the bench)
  • Proper use of colour-coded chopping boards

5. Premises and Equipment
Under Standard 3.2.3, your premises must be designed and maintained to minimise food safety risks. The EHO will check:

  • Floors, walls, and ceilings in good condition, easy to clean
  • Adequate ventilation and lighting
  • Equipment in good working order
  • Plumbing and drainage adequate for your size of operation
  • Adequate storage for food off the floor and away from chemicals

Step 2: Prepare Your Documentation

Gathering your paperwork before the inspection shows the EHO that you take food safety seriously. Create a compliance folder (physical or digital) containing:

  • Your current HACCP-based food safety program
  • Temperature monitoring logs for the last 3 months (at minimum)
  • Cleaning schedules and sign-off sheets
  • Pest control service reports (current certificate)
  • Training certificates for all food handlers and your Food Safety Supervisor
  • Equipment service and calibration records
  • Supplier approval records and delivery receipts
  • Corrective action and incident reports

Step 3: Do a Pre-Inspection Walk-Through

Before the EHO arrives, walk through your premises as if you were inspecting it yourself. Use our free audit readiness checklist to guide you. Pay special attention to:

  • Are all fridges and freezers maintaining temperature and free of frost build-up?
  • Are all food items labelled and date-marked?
  • Are chemicals stored away from food in a designated chemical cupboard?
  • Are hand-washing stations clear of clutter and stocked?
  • Is there any visible pest activity (droppings, gnaw marks, insect bodies)?
  • Are all staff wearing clean uniforms and appropriate hair restraints?

Fixing these issues before the inspection prevents them from becoming non-compliances on the EHO's report.

Common Non-Compliances in NSW Council Inspections

Based on NSW Food Authority data and our own audit experience, the most frequently cited non-compliances include:

  • Inadequate temperature control – cool rooms running above 5°C, hot food held below 60°C, or no temperature records
  • No food safety program or a program that is generic, unsigned, and clearly unrelated to the actual business
  • Poor personal hygiene practices – staff not washing hands, wearing jewellery, or failing to use gloves correctly
  • Dirty premises – grease build-up, mould in cool rooms, dirty equipment, or inadequate cleaning schedules
  • Pest issues – gaps in walls or doors, no pest control contract, or evidence of cockroaches, rodents, or flies
  • Lack of Food Safety Supervisor – the nominated supervisor is not reasonably available or their certification has expired
  • No allergen management – especially important now that Standard 3.2.2A is in full effect

What Happens If You Get a Non-Compliance?

Not every non-compliance means a fine. EHOs have a range of enforcement options:

  • Verbal advice – for minor issues, the EHO may simply advise you to fix it
  • Improvement notice – a formal written notice requiring specific action within a timeframe
  • Prohibition order – stopping a specific activity (e.g., cooking) until the issue is resolved
  • Closure order – shutting the business down immediately if there is an imminent public health risk
  • Penalty infringement notice (fine) – issued for serious or repeated breaches
  • Prosecution – in the most serious cases, matters can go to court

Most EHOs prefer education over enforcement. If you can demonstrate that you are genuinely trying to comply and have a plan to fix the issues, you will generally receive an improvement notice rather than a fine. That said, repeat non-compliance or deliberate neglect will result in escalating penalties.

How Often Do Inspections Happen?

Inspection frequency depends on your risk classification. Under the NSW Food Authority's Risk Classification Scheme, food businesses are rated as:

  • Low risk – inspected every 2–4 years (e.g., businesses selling only pre-packaged low-risk food)
  • Medium risk – inspected every 12–18 months (most cafes, restaurants, and bakeries)
  • High risk – inspected every 6–12 months (businesses handling large volumes of high-risk food, manufacturers, hospitals, aged care)

Your local council will also conduct unannounced inspections in response to complaints or food poisoning incidents. Being prepared every day is the only sustainable approach.

Making Food Safety Part of Your Daily Routine

The businesses that pass inspections effortlessly are the ones that have embedded food safety into their daily operations. They do not scramble to get ready when the EHO calls. They already have the records, the practices, and the culture in place.

Simple daily habits make all the difference:

  • Check and record fridge temperatures at the start of each shift
  • Clean as you go, rather than leaving it all for end of day
  • Date-mark everything as soon as it comes out of delivery
  • Train your team on one food safety topic per week (see our training page)
  • Conduct a brief weekly walk-through using our checklist

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Can the council inspect my business without giving notice?
Yes. In NSW, routine inspections are usually scheduled, but EHOs have the legal right to enter and inspect your premises at any reasonable time without prior notice. They can also enter immediately if they suspect a serious public health risk. The best defense is to run your business as if an inspection is happening tomorrow.
What should I do during an inspection?
Stay calm, be cooperative, and answer questions honestly. If you do not know the answer, say so rather than guessing. Provide the EHO with a clean workspace to review your documents. Accompany them through the premises, take notes on any findings, and ask clarifying questions if you do not understand a requirement. The EHO is there to help you comply, not to shut you down.
How long does a council inspection take?
Most routine inspections take 30 to 90 minutes, depending on the size and complexity of your business. A small cafe might take 30 minutes. A large restaurant with a full kitchen, dry store, and cool rooms could take over an hour. Allow adequate time and do not schedule the inspection during peak service periods if you can avoid it.
