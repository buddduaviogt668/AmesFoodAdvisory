Supplier Approval and Verification: A Complete Guide for NSW Food Businesses

Under Standard 3.2.2 (Food Safety Practices and General Requirements) and Standard 3.2.1 (Food Safety Programs) of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code, every food business in NSW must take all practicable measures to ensure the food they receive, store, process, and sell is safe and suitable. A robust supplier approval and verification program is not a regulatory extra—it is a fundamental legal obligation and a cornerstone of any effective HACCP plan.

This guide covers the full supplier lifecycle: approving new suppliers, conducting receiving checks, maintaining verification records, and performing supplier audits. Whether you run a small cafe or a large manufacturer, these practices will protect your business, your customers, and your regulatory standing.

Why an Approved Supplier Program Matters

An approved supplier program is your first line of defence against food safety incidents. If a supplier delivers contaminated ingredients, your own food safety controls—no matter how rigorous—may be overwhelmed. The NSW Food Authority and local councils routinely check supplier approval records during inspections and audits. A business that cannot demonstrate an approved supplier list risks enforcement action, including penalty infringement notices (PINs) and prosecution under the Food Act 2003 (NSW).

Beyond compliance, a formal supplier approval program reduces the risk of allergen cross-contact, microbiological contamination, incorrect labelling, and supply chain fraud. It also strengthens your position if you ever need to conduct a trace-back or recall. For businesses operating under a licensed Food Safety Program, supplier approval is a mandatory supporting program under SQF or HACCP Certification.

Step-by-Step Supplier Approval Process

A robust supplier approval process follows a clear, documented procedure. Below is the framework we recommend for NSW food businesses:

  • Step 1 – Supplier Questionnaire: Send each prospective supplier a food safety questionnaire covering their licences, certifications (e.g. SQF, BRC, GFSI-benchmarked schemes), third-party audit reports, allergen management, and recall history.
  • Step 2 – Document Review: Review certificates of incorporation, public liability insurance, any relevant NSW Food Authority licences, and third-party audit certificates. Verify that the supplier’s scope matches what you intend to purchase.
  • Step 3 – Risk Categorisation: Assign a risk level (low, medium, high) based on the ingredient type. For example, raw poultry is high-risk; dried spices may be medium-risk; packaging materials may be low-risk. High-risk suppliers require site audits before approval.
  • Step 4 – Site Audit (High-Risk Suppliers): Conduct an on-site supplier audit using a standardised checklist that covers GMP, cleaning, pest control, traceability, allergen controls, and temperature monitoring. Our internal auditing services can support this process.
  • Step 5 – Approval Decision: Document the approval decision, including any conditions (e.g. “approved with conditions pending corrective actions”). Maintain an approved supplier register with dates and reviewer names.
  • Step 6 – Onboarding: Provide the supplier with your specifications, including microbiological limits, packaging requirements, labelling requirements, and delivery temperature tolerances.

Receiving Checks: What to Inspect on Every Delivery

No matter how thorough your approval process, receiving checks are non-negotiable. Standard 3.2.2 requires that food businesses not accept food that is unsafe or unsuitable. Every delivery must be checked against a receiving checklist. Key checks include:

  • Temperature: Record the temperature of potentially hazardous foods at receipt. Chilled foods should arrive at 5°C or below; frozen foods should be frozen hard (ideally -18°C or below). Hot-held foods (for RTE products) should be at 60°C or above.
  • Packaging Integrity: Inspect for tears, punctures, swelling, leaks, pest damage, or signs of tampering. Reject any compromised packaging.
  • Use-by / Best-before Dates: Verify that shelf life is adequate for your intended use. Reject product with less than 75% of its shelf life remaining (or your internal policy).
  • Labelling: Confirm the product matches your purchase order and that allergen declarations, ingredient lists, and country of origin labelling are present and correct. See our guide on food labelling requirements.
  • Vehicle Condition: Check the delivery vehicle for cleanliness, odour, pest evidence, and proper refrigeration function.
  • Documentation: Collect delivery dockets, invoices, and any required certificates of analysis (COAs) or certificates of conformance.

All receiving checks must be recorded on a receiving log. These records must be retained for at least the shelf life of the product plus a reasonable period (NSW Food Authority recommends a minimum of 12 months, or longer for licensed businesses). Our SOP templates include receiving check templates you can adapt.

Verification Records and Document Retention

Verification records are the evidence that your supplier approval program is working. Under the Food Standards Code, records must be created at the time the activity occurs and be legible, accurate, and permanent. Key verification records include:

  • Approved supplier register (with risk ratings and approval dates)
  • Supplier questionnaires and returned documentation
  • Receiving check logs (temperature, condition, date checks)
  • Certificate of analysis (COA) for high-risk ingredients
  • Supplier audit reports and corrective action close-outs
  • Non-conformance reports (NCRs) raised against suppliers
  • Supplier performance scorecards (if used)

In NSW, the Food Regulation 2015 requires that food safety records be retained for at least 12 months (or longer for licensed businesses under specific licence conditions). We recommend retaining supplier approval records for the duration of the trading relationship plus three years for traceability purposes. Supplier Audits and Ongoing Monitoring

Supplier approval is not a once-off event. Your program must include ongoing monitoring and periodic re-verification. We recommend the following schedule:

  • High-risk suppliers: On-site audit annually; document review every 6 months.
  • Medium-risk suppliers: Desktop audit (document review) annually; on-site audit every 2 years.
  • Low-risk suppliers: Document review every 2 years; on-site audit as needed.

Supplier audits should assess ongoing compliance against your specifications. Areas to cover include: GMP and hygiene, cleaning and sanitation, pest control, allergen management, traceability exercises, temperature control, training records, and corrective action history. Use a standardised audit checklist to ensure consistency. Our internal audit service can conduct supplier audits on your behalf, providing impartial assessments that strengthen your supply chain.

Supplier non-conformances must be documented and followed up with corrective action requests (CARs). If a supplier repeatedly fails to meet requirements, de-listing and sourcing an alternative supplier should be considered. Maintain a de-listed supplier register with reasons for de-listing.

NSW Regulatory Context and Enforcement

The NSW Food Authority enforces food safety requirements across the state, including those relating to supplier approval and verification. During an inspection or audit, an authorised officer may request to see your approved supplier register, receiving records, and any supplier audit documentation. Failure to maintain these records can result in penalty notices (up to $1,320 for an individual and $6,600 for a corporation under the Food Act 2003 (NSW)) or prosecution for more serious breaches.

For businesses operating under a Food Safety Program (licensable activities such as catering, manufacturing, and food service to vulnerable populations), supplier approval is explicitly required under Standard 3.2.1. Your Food Safety Program must describe your supplier approval procedures, and evidence of implementation must be available at all times.

If you are unsure whether your current supplier approval program meets NSW regulatory requirements, consider engaging a food safety consultant to conduct a gap analysis.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

What records must I keep for supplier approval in NSW?
At a minimum, you must maintain an approved supplier register, receiving check records (including temperatures), and documentation of any supplier audits or reviews. Records should be retained for at least 12 months. Licensed businesses under Standard 3.2.1 must keep all records related to their Food Safety Program, including supplier approval, for the duration specified in their licence conditions.
Do I need to audit every supplier?
Not necessarily. A risk-based approach is recommended. High-risk suppliers (raw meat, poultry, dairy, seafood) should be audited on-site at least annually. Lower-risk suppliers (packaging, dry goods) may be verified through document review. Your approved supplier program should define the risk criteria and the corresponding verification frequency.
Can I use a supplier’s third-party certification instead of conducting my own audit?
Yes. If a supplier holds a GFSI-benchmarked certification (such as SQF, BRCGS, or FSSC 22000), you may accept their third-party audit certificate in lieu of conducting your own on-site audit, provided the scope of their certification covers the products you are purchasing. You should still verify that the certificate is current and request a copy of the audit report (or at least the scope and non-conformance summary).
