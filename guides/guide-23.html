Food Safety Record Keeping Requirements NSW

Effective record keeping is the backbone of every food safety management system. Learn exactly what records your NSW food business must keep, for how long, and how to build a defensible audit trail.

Why Record Keeping Is a Legal Requirement in NSW

Under the Food Standards Code Standard 3.2.2 (Division 5 â€” Record Keeping), all NSW food businesses are required to keep specific records. These records are not optional paperwork â€” they are your primary evidence that you are operating safely, managing risks, and complying with the law. Without adequate records, you cannot prove due diligence. In an audit or enforcement action, if it is not written down, it did not happen.

Good record keeping also delivers operational benefits: trend analysis helps you identify recurring problems, temperature logs protect you in supplier disputes, and training records demonstrate a competent workforce. What Records Must Your NSW Food Business Keep?

The specific records required depend on the nature of your business, but the following categories apply to nearly every food business in NSW:

1. Temperature Control Records

Temperature monitoring is the single most frequently audited record type. You must document:

  • Receiving temperatures: The temperature of potentially hazardous foods when they arrive at your premises. For chilled foods, this must be â‰¤ 5Â°C unless an alternative temperature is justified in your Food Safety Program. Frozen foods should be frozen hard with no evidence of thawing.
  • Storage temperatures: At least twice-daily checks of cool rooms, fridges, and freezers (opening and closing times). Fridges â‰¤ 5Â°C, freezers â‰¤ -15Â°C (or -18Â°C for ice cream).
  • Cooking temperatures: Core temperature of cooked potentially hazardous foods must reach at least 75Â°C (or a validated alternative time-temperature combination).
  • Cooling temperatures: If you cool and reheat food, you must document the cooling curve: from 60Â°C to 21Â°C within 2 hours, then from 21Â°C to 5Â°C within a further 4 hours (total 6 hours).
  • Transport temperatures: If you transport potentially hazardous foods, temperature records must accompany each delivery. See our food transportation safety guide for detailed requirements.
  • Reheating temperatures: Reheat to 60Â°C or above within 2 hours if food was cooled and stored correctly.

2. Cleaning and Sanitation Records

Your cleaning schedule must be documented and the completion of each task recorded. Include:

  • Daily cleaning tasks (e.g., wiping down food contact surfaces, sweeping floors).
  • Weekly tasks (e.g., deep cleaning ovens, degreasing extraction hoods).
  • Periodic tasks (e.g., cleaning cool room condensers, grease trap servicing).
  • Chemical concentration checks: sanitiser solutions (e.g., quaternary ammonium compounds or chlorine) must be checked with test strips and the results recorded.
  • Verification activities: ATP swabbing or environmental swab results, if applicable.

3. Training Records

Every food handler must have training appropriate to their role. Your training records should include:

  • Individual training matrix showing each staff member's completed training.
  • Certificates of completion for: Food Safety Supervisor certification (mandatory in NSW), allergen management training, and any role-specific training.
  • Induction checklist for new staff covering: personal hygiene, hand washing, temperature control, allergen awareness, and your Food Safety Program.
  • Ongoing training logs: refresher sessions, toolbox talks, and corrective action retraining.

4. Supplier and Receival Records

You must be able to trace every ingredient back to its source. Maintain:

  • An approved supplier register with supplier details, audit status (if applicable), and date of approval.
  • Receival records including: date, product description, supplier name, quantity, batch/lot number, temperature on receipt, and sign-off by the receiving person.
  • Specifications for all ingredients, particularly for allergens and raw materials that impact your CCPs.

5. Pest Control Records

  • Current pest control contract with a licensed service provider.
  • Pest control service reports (monthly for most businesses).
  • Bait station map showing numbered locations throughout the premises.
  • Pest sighting log â€” any staff sighting of pests must be documented.
  • Corrective actions taken in response to pest activity.

6. Corrective Action and Incident Records

Every time a critical limit is breached or an incident occurs, you must document it. See our detailed guide to corrective action procedures for full requirements. Key records include:

  • Corrective Action Reports (CARs) with root cause analysis.
  • Incident reports (e.g., customer allergic reaction, foreign matter complaint).
  • Food recall and traceability records (mock recall exercises should be conducted at least annually).

7. Food Safety Program Documents

Your Food Safety Program is a living document. Keep version-controlled copies of:

  • The current HACCP plan (or Food Safety Program) with signed approval.
  • All supporting documents: process flow diagrams, hazard analysis tables, CCP determination and monitoring procedures.
  • Previous versions for audit trail purposes.
  • Third-party certifications (SQF, BRC, etc.) if applicable.

How Long Must You Keep Food Safety Records in NSW?

The retention period depends on the record type:

  • Standard 3.2.2 records (temperature logs, cleaning records, corrective actions): Minimum 6 months from the date the record was made.
  • Food Safety Program: Current version plus at least the previous version â€” indefinitely.
  • Training records: For the duration of employment plus 6 months after the employee leaves.
  • Supplier and traceability records: Minimum 6 months, but we recommend at least 12 months to cover audit cycles.
  • Pest control records: Minimum 12 months (many councils require 2 years).
  • Third-party certification records: For the certification cycle plus one full audit cycle.

Records can be kept in hard copy or electronic format, but they must be readily retrievable. If an inspector requests records, you must be able to produce them immediately. Cloud-based systems are becoming the norm and offer the advantage of automated backups and easy searchability.

Building a Defensible Audit Trail

An audit trail is not just about having records â€” it is about records that are consistent, legible, and complete. Here is how to build a trail that auditors trust:

  • Use standardised forms: Every staff member should use the same template. Loose scraps of paper with scribbled temperatures are not acceptable.
  • Complete every field: If a field is not applicable, write "N/A" â€” do not leave it blank.
  • Sign and date everything: Every entry must be signed (or initialled) by the person who performed the task.
  • Correct errors properly: Never use white-out. Draw a single line through the error, initial it, and write the correct value above.
  • Review records regularly: The Food Safety Supervisor should review temperature logs weekly and sign them off. Anomalies should be flagged and investigated.
  • Use digital systems where possible: Electronic records with timestamps and user authentication are harder to dispute and easier to search.

Common Record-Keeping Mistakes NSW Businesses Make

  • Pre-printed or "anticipated" log entries: Filling in tomorrow's temperature values today is fraudulent and will destroy your credibility with an auditor.
  • Switching between Celsius and Fahrenheit: Always use Celsius. An accidental Fahrenheit reading of 40Â°F = 4.4Â°C may look fine, but 40Â°C would be a critical breach.
  • Inconsistent record keeping: Logs from three months ago are detailed, but the past month has gaps. Auditors will question whether the process is truly in control.
  • No verification of digital records: Electronic temperature probes and data loggers must be calibrated â€” keep the calibration certificates with your records.
  • Food Safety Program not updated: If you changed your menu, equipment, or process flow but did not update the FSP, your records will not match reality.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep my food safety records electronically instead of on paper?
Yes, absolutely. The NSW Food Authority accepts electronic records provided they are accurate, legible, and secure. Electronic systems offer advantages: automated temperature logging, tamper-proof timestamps, and easy searchability. However, you must ensure backups are maintained and records can be produced immediately upon request. If using a cloud-based system, confirm the provider's data is hosted within Australia or meets Australian privacy requirements.
What happens if I lose my food safety records in a fire or flood?
You are still expected to maintain records. Cloud-based backups are the best protection. If records are lost, you should document the loss (with photos or an incident report) and begin rebuilding immediately. In an audit, the loss of records due to a genuine disaster will be treated more leniently than systematic record-keeping failure, but you must demonstrate that you did have a record-keeping system in place before the loss occurred.
Do I need to keep records for foods that I donate to charity?
Yes. Food donations are still classified as food for sale under the Food Standards Code. Temperature logs, traceability records, and labelling must be maintained for donated food just as they are for food sold to paying customers. The NSW Food Authority has published specific guidance on food donation â€” if you regularly donate food, we recommend integrating this into your Food Safety Program.
