What Is HACCP? A Complete Guide for NSW Food Businesses

If you run a food business in NSW, you have likely heard the term HACCP. But what does it actually mean, and do you need one? HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point. It is a systematic, science-based approach to identifying, evaluating, and controlling food safety hazards. Unlike a one-off checklist, HACCP is a preventive system designed to stop hazards before they reach the consumer.

In Australia, HACCP is referenced under Standard 3.2.1 of the FSANZ Food Standards Code, which requires all food businesses to have a food safety program. The NSW Food Authority enforces these requirements across the state, and your local council will check for HACCP-based controls during routine inspections. Whether you run a cafe in Surry Hills, a catering business in Newcastle, or a manufacturing facility in Western Sydney, understanding HACCP is non-negotiable.

Who Needs HACCP in NSW?

Under the Food Act 2003 (NSW) and the Food Regulation 2015, all food businesses in NSW that handle, process, or serve food must operate under a documented food safety program that is based on HACCP principles. This includes:

  • Restaurants, cafes, and takeaway shops
  • Caterers and mobile food vendors
  • Childcare centres, aged care facilities, and hospitals
  • Retail bakeries and butchers
  • Supermarkets and grocery stores with deli sections
  • Food manufacturers and processors
  • Wholesale distributors and importers

Businesses that only sell whole, uncut fruit and vegetables, or pre-packaged shelf-stable foods, may be exempt. However, if you slice, cook, cool, reheat, or combine ingredients, you fall under the requirement. Check with the NSW Food Authority if you are unsure about your specific classification.

The Seven HACCP Principles

The Codex Alimentarius Commission defines seven principles that form the backbone of every HACCP system. Understanding each one is essential for developing a compliant food safety program.

Principle 1: Conduct a Hazard Analysis
Identify all biological, chemical, and physical hazards that could occur at each step of your process. For a typical NSW café, this might include biological hazards (Salmonella from raw chicken), chemical hazards (cleaning agent residues), and physical hazards (glass fragments from broken jars).

Principle 2: Determine Critical Control Points (CCPs)
A CCP is a step where control is essential to prevent or eliminate a hazard. Cooking raw chicken to 74°C is a CCP. So is cooling cooked rice below 5°C within two hours.

Principle 3: Establish Critical Limits
Each CCP must have a measurable limit. For cooking, the critical limit might be an internal temperature of 74°C for two minutes. For cold storage, it could be 5°C or below.

Principle 4: Establish Monitoring Procedures
You need to know, in real time, whether your CCPs are under control. This means taking and recording temperatures at defined frequencies. A written SOP for temperature monitoring ensures consistency across all shifts.

Principle 5: Establish Corrective Actions
When a critical limit is breached, you need a plan. If your cool room hits 8°C, what do you do? Corrective actions might include moving food to a functioning cool room, rejecting the affected batch, or recording the deviation and disposal.

Principle 6: Establish Verification Procedures
Verification confirms your HACCP system is working. This includes reviewing temperature logs, calibrating thermometers, and conducting internal audits.

Principle 7: Establish Record-Keeping and Documentation
If it is not documented, it did not happen. Your records must include hazard analyses, CCP monitoring logs, corrective action reports, and verification results. The NSW Food Authority expects to see these records during an inspection.

HACCP vs Food Safety Program: What Is the Difference?

This is one of the most common questions we hear. A food safety program is the documented system that your business uses to control food safety hazards. HACCP is the methodology that underpins it. Think of it this way: your food safety program is the document; HACCP is the science behind it.

Under Standard 3.2.1 of the FSANZ Code, your food safety program must be based on HACCP principles, but it does not need to be formally certified to Codex HACCP unless you are supplying major retailers or seeking third-party certification like SQF or BRC. Most small to medium NSW food businesses operate with a HACCP-based food safety program without formal certification, and that is perfectly acceptable under NSW law.

Standard 3.2.2A and What It Means for Your HACCP System

In December 2023, the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code introduced Standard 3.2.2A, which mandates additional food safety management tools for certain sectors. If you handle high-risk foods (raw meat, poultry, eggs, dairy, seafood, cooked rice, etc.), you are now required to implement:

  • A documented food safety management system (your HACCP-based program)
  • Evidence of food handler training (such as a Food Safety Supervisor certification)
  • A documented allergen management process

This standard applies to food service, retail, and hospitality businesses in NSW. Your HACCP plan must now explicitly address allergen cross-contact as a hazard something many older plans overlook. Our Allergen Management Plan Guide covers this in detail.

Common HACCP Mistakes We See in NSW Food Businesses

Over the years, we have reviewed dozens of food safety programs and conducted countless internal audits. Here are the most common gaps we see:

  • Generic, templated programs that do not match the actual business. A HACCP plan for a bakery is completely different from one for a Thai restaurant. Your plan must reflect your specific menu, equipment, and processes.
  • No flow diagram. Your food safety program should include a step-by-step flow diagram of how food moves through your business, from receiving to serving.
  • Missing corrective action records. It is not enough to have a policy. You must demonstrate that you have used it.
  • Uncalibrated thermometers. Your temperature readings are meaningless if your equipment is inaccurate.
  • No consideration of allergens. With Standard 3.2.2A now in effect, allergen cross-contact must be treated as a hazard in your analysis.

How to Get HACCP-Ready: A Practical Checklist

Ready to build or improve your HACCP system? Here is your action plan:

  • Download our free audit readiness checklist to identify gaps in your current system.
  • Map your food processes from receiving to serving with a flow diagram.
  • Identify all hazards at each step and determine your CCPs.
  • Set critical limits that are measurable and defensible.
  • Write monitoring procedures and create log sheets.
  • Train your team on CCP monitoring and corrective actions.
  • Schedule regular internal audits to verify your system.
  • Review and update your plan at least annually, or whenever you change your menu or equipment.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a certified HACCP plan for my cafe?
No. Unless you are seeking third-party certification (SQF, BRC, or GFSI), your food safety program simply needs to be based on HACCP principles. It does not need to be externally certified. The NSW Food Authority will check that your documented program is appropriate for your business and that you are following it.
How often should I review my HACCP plan?
At minimum, annually. However, you should review your plan whenever you introduce a new menu item, change equipment, renovate your kitchen, or receive a non-compliance notice from your council. A living document is far more valuable than a dusty binder.
What happens if I do not have a HACCP-based food safety program?
Operating without a compliant food safety program is an offence under the Food Act 2003 (NSW). Penalties can range from improvement notices and fines to closure orders. Your local council environmental health officer will check for your program during routine inspections, and the absence of one will almost certainly result in enforcement action.
