Temperature Control Requirements for NSW Food Businesses

Temperature control is the single most critical operational control in any food business. Under the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code (FSANZ), specifically Standard 3.2.2 — Food Safety Practices and General Requirements, all NSW food businesses must maintain potentially hazardous food at temperatures that prevent the growth of pathogenic microorganisms. The NSW Food Authority enforces these requirements through routine council inspections, and non-compliance is one of the most common causes of penalty infringement notices across the state.

This guide covers the specific temperature control requirements for cold holding, hot holding, cooling, freezing, transport, thermometer calibration, and record-keeping. Whether you operate a café in Surry Hills, a restaurant in Parramatta, or a food manufacturer in Western Sydney, these rules apply to you.

Cold Holding Requirements (Standard 3.2.2)

Potentially hazardous food that is intended to be stored cold must be kept at 5 °C or below. This applies to all refrigerated storage, including walk-in coolers, under-bench fridges, bain-marie cold wells, and display cabinets. The 5 °C limit is critical because most foodborne pathogens cease to multiply or grow only very slowly below this temperature.

Common cold-holding items include: dairy products, cooked meats, seafood, cut fruit and vegetables, prepared salads, dips, and any food that has been cooked and is being held for later service. Your refrigeration equipment must be capable of maintaining 5 °C even during peak ambient temperature conditions and frequent door openings.

For open cold display (e.g., sushi cabinets, deli displays), you must ensure the cold chain is maintained. The NSW Food Authority recommends food temperature checks at least twice daily and recording those checks in a temperature monitoring log.

Hot Holding Requirements

Potentially hazardous food that is intended to be served hot must be maintained at 60 °C or above. This includes bain-maries, hot cabinets, soup kettles, and heat lamps. If the temperature of hot-held food drops below 60 °C, the “2-hour/4-hour rule” applies (see below).

Food must reach 60 °C within a reasonable timeframe after cooking. Holding food below 60 °C for extended periods creates a danger zone where bacteria such as Bacillus cereus, Staphylococcus aureus, and Clostridium perfringens can produce toxins.

The 2-Hour / 4-Hour Rule

Standard 3.2.2 permits food to be kept between 5 °C and 60 °C (the temperature danger zone) for limited periods under the following conditions:

  • Less than 2 hours: Food can be returned to refrigeration, reheated, or used immediately.
  • Between 2 and 4 hours: Food must be used immediately and cannot be returned to storage.
  • More than 4 hours: Food must be discarded.

This rule is critical for buffet service, catered events, and any situation where food is displayed at ambient temperature. You must have documented processes in place to track time out of temperature control. Our food safety consulting team can help you develop a compliant time-as-a-control system.

Cooling Cooked Food Safely

Improper cooling is one of the leading causes of foodborne illness outbreaks. Standard 3.2.2 requires that if you cook potentially hazardous food and intend to store it for later use, you must cool it to 5 °C as quickly as possible. The accepted industry standard, endorsed by both FSANZ and the NSW Food Authority, is:

  • Cool from 60 °C to 21 °C within 2 hours
  • Cool from 21 °C to 5 °C within a further 4 hours

Effective cooling methods include using shallow pans (no more than 10 cm deep), ice baths, blast chillers, dividing food into smaller portions, and stirring in an ice paddle. Your HACCP plan must include specific cooling procedures validated for your operation.

Freezing and Frozen Storage

While freezing does not kill all microorganisms, it halts their growth. The recommended frozen storage temperature is -18 °C or below. Freezing temperature logs must be maintained, and freezers should have audible or visual alarms for temperature excursions. If a freezer fails and food temperature rises above -18 °C, assess the food quality and safety on a case-by-case basis. Never refreeze food that has fully thawed unless it has been cooked first.

Thermometer Calibration and Accuracy

Your temperature measurements are only as reliable as your thermometer. The NSW Food Authority requires that all food businesses use a calibrated probe thermometer with an accuracy of ±1 °C. Calibration must be performed at least monthly and any time the thermometer is dropped or exposed to extreme temperatures. Calibration methods include:

  • Ice-point method: Insert probe into a 50/50 mixture of crushed ice and water. It should read 0 °C.
  • Boiling-point method: Insert probe into boiling water. It should read 100 °C (adjust for altitude).

Records of all calibrations must be kept as part of your food safety documentation. Download our free thermometer calibration log or engage our internal auditing services to verify your temperature monitoring system is compliant.

Temperature Monitoring Records

The NSW Food Authority expects to see temperature monitoring records during any routine inspection. Your records should document:

  • Date and time of each temperature check
  • Product name and batch/lot identifier
  • Temperature reading (°C)
  • Corrective action taken if temperature was out of range
  • Staff member who performed the check

Temperature logs must be kept for at least 12 months or longer if your local council requires it. Digital logging systems with automated alerts are increasingly recommended for medium and high-risk businesses. For help designing a temperature monitoring system tailored to your operation, explore our food safety training programs or book a site visit.

Temperature Control During Transport

If your business transports potentially hazardous food, Standard 3.2.2 also applies during transit. Food transport vehicles must maintain the required temperatures (5 °C or below for cold, 60 °C or above for hot). Use insulated containers, refrigerated vehicles, or temperature-controlled packaging. Record temperature at the point of dispatch and upon receipt at the destination.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the maximum temperature for cold food storage in NSW?

Under Standard 3.2.2 of the FSANZ Code, potentially hazardous food must be stored at 5 °C or below. Some exemptions apply for specific products (e.g., hard cheeses, whole fresh produce), but as a general rule all refrigerated potentially hazardous food must be at or below 5 °C.

Do I need to log temperatures on paper or can I use a digital system?

Both paper and digital systems are acceptable under NSW legislation, provided records are accurate, legible, and retained for at least 12 months. Digital systems with automated sensors and cloud logging are strongly recommended for traceability and audit readiness. The NSW Food Authority accepts digital records during inspections.

What should I do if my fridge breaks down?

Immediately assess the internal temperature of all stored food. Food that has been above 5 °C for less than 2 hours can be relocated to a functioning fridge. If the temperature has exceeded 5 °C for more than 2 hours, check using the 2-hour/4-hour rule. Food above 5 °C for more than 4 hours must be discarded. Contact a refrigeration technician immediately and document all corrective actions for your food safety records.
