Food Labelling Requirements in Australia – Complete Guide

Food labelling is one of the most heavily regulated areas of the food industry in Australia. The requirements are set out in FSANZ Part 1.2 of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code (Standards 1.2.1 through 1.2.12), enforced in NSW by the NSW Food Authority and local councils under the Food Act 2003 (NSW) and the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth).

Whether you are a new food business starting up or an established manufacturer updating a product range, this guide covers everything you need to know: allergen labelling, country of origin, date marking, nutrition panels, and common compliance pitfalls specific to NSW.

Overview of FSANZ Part 1.2 Labelling Standards

Part 1.2 of the Food Standards Code covers the labelling and information requirements for all food sold in Australia. The key standards include:

  • Standard 1.2.1 – Application of labelling and information requirements
  • Standard 1.2.2 – Information requirements – food identification (name, lot identification, supplier address)
  • Standard 1.2.3 – Mandatory Warning and Advisory Statements and Declarations (allergens, added sulphites, etc.)
  • Standard 1.2.4 – Information requirements – percentage labelling (characterising ingredients)
  • Standard 1.2.5 – Date marking (use-by and best-before dates)
  • Standard 1.2.6 – Direction for use and storage
  • Standard 1.2.7 – Nutrition, health and related claims
  • Standard 1.2.8 – Nutrition information requirements (Nutrition Information Panel)
  • Standard 1.2.11 – Country of origin labelling

Every food label sold in NSW must comply with these standards. Non-compliance can result in penalty notices, product recalls, and prosecution. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) also enforces country of origin and false/misleading representation requirements under Australian Consumer Law.

Allergen Labelling Requirements

Standard 1.2.3 requires that any food containing one of the following allergens must declare it on the label in bold in the ingredient list:

  • Peanuts, tree nuts (e.g. almonds, cashews, walnuts)
  • Milk, eggs, fish, crustaceans, molluscs
  • Soybeans, lupin
  • Sesame seeds, sulphites (added in concentrations of 10 mg/kg or more)
  • Cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats, spelt, triticale)

Allergen declarations must be clear and unambiguous. The food industry has widely adopted the “Contains” statement (e.g. “Contains milk, wheat, egg”) as best practice, though it is not mandatory by law if allergens are declared in the ingredient list in bold. Precautionary allergen labelling (PAL) statements such as “may contain traces of” should only be used when there is a genuine risk of cross-contact that cannot be eliminated through GMP. Over-use of PAL statements devalues their meaning and may still expose you to liability.

For businesses operating under a HACCP-based Food Safety Program, allergen management must extend beyond labelling to include receiving checks, storage separation, production scheduling, and cleaning validation. Incorrect allergen labelling is the single leading cause of food recalls in Australia.

Country of Origin Labelling

Standard 1.2.11 and the Competition and Consumer (Country of Origin) Information Instrument 2021 set out the rules for country of origin labelling. For food grown, produced, or made in Australia, you may use the kangaroo-in-a-triangle logo. The key phrases are:

  • “Product of Australia” – At least 100% of ingredients are Australian, and virtually all processing occurs in Australia.
  • “Made in Australia” – At least 50% of the cost of production is Australian, and the food is substantially transformed here.
  • “Made in Australia from local and imported ingredients” – Used when a mix of Australian and imported ingredients is used.
  • “Imported” – Must state the country of origin (e.g. “Product of Italy”).

The ACCC enforces these rules and has issued significant penalties for false country of origin claims. If you are sourcing from multiple suppliers, verify the origin claims your suppliers make and retain supporting documentation.

Date Marking

Standard 1.2.5 requires that most packaged food be date-marked. Two types of dates are recognised:

  • Use-by date: Required for microbiologically high-risk foods (e.g. fresh meat, poultry, dairy, sliced meats). After this date, the food is not safe to eat, and it is illegal to sell it. Use-by dates must be determined through scientific shelf-life testing.
  • Best-before date: Used for foods with a longer shelf life (e.g. canned goods, biscuits, spices). After this date, the food may still be safe but quality may have declined. It is legal to sell food past its best-before date, provided it is still safe and suitable.

The label must also include storage instructions if the food requires specific conditions (e.g. “Refrigerate below 5°C”, “Store in a cool, dry place”). Our SOP templates include shelf-life verification and date-checking procedures.

Nutrition Information Panel (NIP)

Standard 1.2.8 requires that most packaged foods display a Nutrition Information Panel (NIP) showing:

  • Energy (kJ), protein, total fat, saturated fat, carbohydrate, sugars, and sodium (per serving and per 100g/mL)
  • Any other nutrient for which a claim is made (e.g. fibre, calcium)
  • Quantities of saturated fat, sugars, and sodium can also be expressed as a percentage of daily intake (%DI) per serve

The NIP must be based on an accurate analysis of the product. This can be achieved through laboratory testing or using a reputable nutrition analysis software based on validated Australian food composition data. If you make health claims (e.g. “low fat”, “good source of calcium”), you must also comply with Standard 1.2.7 (Nutrition, Health and Related Claims).

Small businesses are not exempt from labelling requirements, though there are limited exemptions for very small packages (surface area less than 100 cm²) and for food sold at fundraisers or by charity organisations. However, if you are selling to the public through retail or food service, the labelling rules apply. Contact AMES Food Advisory for a labelling compliance review.

Additional NSW-Specific Requirements

The NSW Food Authority may impose additional labelling conditions for licensed businesses through their Food Safety Program. For example, businesses serving vulnerable populations (e.g. aged care, childcare, hospitals) may need to include specific allergen warnings on menus or buffet items. The Food Act 2003 (NSW) also prohibits false or misleading descriptions, including claims about nutritional content, health benefits, or geographic origin.

For unlabelled food (e.g. bakery display items, deli counter products), the information must still be available to the customer on request or through point-of-sale materials. Allergen information must be readily accessible for all unpackaged food.

Labelling Exemptions and Special Cases

Certain foods are exempt from full labelling requirements under the Food Standards Code. These include:

  • Food sold at fundraising events (limited exemption for charities and community organisations, provided certain conditions are met)
  • Food in very small packages (surface area less than 100 cm² may be exempt from the NIP, but allergen and name/address information is still required)
  • Food prepared and sold at the point of sale (e.g. a cafe making sandwiches for sale that day – labelling requirements are reduced but allergen information must still be available)
  • Whole or cut fresh fruit and vegetables (exempt from most labelling requirements unless pre-packaged)

Exemptions are narrowly interpreted. If you are unsure whether your product qualifies, seek legal or consultancy advice. Contact AMES Food Advisory for a label compliance assessment.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Nutrition Information Panel if I only sell at farmers’ markets?
Generally, yes. If you are selling packaged food to the public at farmers’ markets, you must comply with the same labelling requirements as retail stores. However, if your business qualifies as a charity or community organisation selling food at a fundraising event, narrow exemptions may apply. Verify your status with your local council or the NSW Food Authority.
What happens if I label a product incorrectly in NSW?
Consequences range from a warning letter to penalty infringement notices (up to $1,320 for individuals, $6,600 for corporations) under the Food Act 2003 (NSW). Incorrect allergen labelling carries the additional risk of causing a serious allergic reaction, potentially leading to civil liability. If a labelling error is found, the NSW Food Authority may require a recall, which can be extremely costly for a small business.
Do home-based food businesses need labelled packaging?
Yes. If you are operating a home-based food business in NSW (which must be notified with your local council), all packaged food must carry a label compliant with FSANZ Part 1.2. There is no exemption for home-based production. The label must include the business name and address, food name, ingredient list, allergen declarations, date marking, and a NIP where required.
