Food Safety for Bakeries & Patisseries NSW

Bakeries and patisseries face unique food safety challenges — from egg and dairy temperature control to allergen cross-contact in open kitchens. This guide covers specific NSW compliance requirements for your bakery.

Why Bakeries Face Unique Food Safety Challenges

Bakeries and patisseries occupy a distinct space in the food safety landscape. You work with high-risk ingredients — eggs, dairy, cream, custards, and sometimes raw flour — in an environment where cross-contact is a constant challenge. Your products are often displayed openly, handled multiple times during decorating and finishing, and sold both fresh and packaged with varying shelf lives. Under the Food Standards Code Standard 3.2.2 and Standard 3.2.3, every bakery in NSW must have a Food Safety Program that addresses these specific risks.

Whether you run a neighbourhood bakery in Paddington, a wholesale patisserie supplying cafés across Sydney, or a gluten-free artisan bakery in the Blue Mountains, your compliance approach must be tailored to your products and processes. Our HACCP plan development service specialises in bakery and patisserie operations.

Specific Bakery Compliance Under NSW Regulations

The NSW Food Authority classifies bakeries as handling potentially hazardous foods when you work with dairy, eggs, or cooked products that require temperature control. Key areas of focus include:

Food Safety Supervisor Requirements

Under NSW legislation, every bakery that handles potentially hazardous unpackaged food must appoint at least one trained Food Safety Supervisor. This person must hold a nationally recognised training qualification (e.g., Certificate III in Food Processing or a Food Safety Supervisor certificate from a registered training organisation) and be reasonably available at all times the business is operating. The certificate must be displayed on the premises. Our food safety training courses provide the certification your supervisor needs.

Food Safety Program for Bakeries

Your Food Safety Program must be specific to your bakery's processes. It should include HACCP-based controls for each step: receival, storage, mixing, proofing, baking, cooling, finishing (icing, filling, decorating), and display. Common CCPs in bakery operations include:

  • Cooking/baking temperature (CCP) — core temperature for meat pies, quiches, and egg-based products.
  • Cooling rate (CCP) — particularly for custards, cream fillings, and large baked goods that cool slowly.
  • Storage temperature (CCP) — chilled storage for cream, custards, dairy-based icings, and egg washes.
  • Display temperature (CCP) — ambient display for shelf-stable goods, refrigerated display for potentially hazardous items.

Allergen Cross-Contact in Bakeries: The Critical Risk

Bakeries are among the highest-risk environments for allergen cross-contact. With flour dust in the air, shared equipment, and multiple products passing through the same production line, the potential for trace amounts of allergen to migrate from one product to another is significant. Under the Food Standards Code, you must take all reasonable precautions to avoid the presence of undeclared allergens in your products.

Practical Controls for Bakery Allergen Management

  • Segregation of ingredients: Store allergenic ingredients (nuts, seeds, milk powder, eggs, soy) in separate, clearly labelled containers below and away from non-allergenic ingredients.
  • Dedicated equipment: Where possible, use separate mixing bowls, whisks, baking trays, and decorating tools for allergen-free products. If this is not practical, implement a validated clean-in-place procedure between runs.
  • Production scheduling: Schedule allergen-free products first thing in the morning on clean lines. Follow with products containing allergens. This reduces the risk of cross-contact throughout the day.
  • Cleaning validation: Standard cleaning may not remove allergenic residues from porous surfaces or textured equipment. Use ATP swabbing or allergen-specific test kits to verify cleaning effectiveness.
  • Clear labelling: All pre-packaged products must have a full ingredient list with allergens declared in bold (per Food Standards Code Standard 1.2.3). For unpackaged products sold from a display cabinet, provide allergen information in writing at the point of sale.
  • Staff training: Every team member must understand the difference between allergen cross-contact (allergen protein transfer) and food spoilage, and the potentially fatal consequences for allergic customers. Our training programs include dedicated allergen management modules.

For detailed allergen management procedures, we can develop bespoke SOPs for your bakery that account for your specific equipment, layout, and product range.

Date Marking in Bakeries: Getting It Right

Date marking is a common source of non-conformance in bakery audits. Under Standard 3.2.2 (Clause 8), packaged food with a shelf life of less than 2 years must bear a date mark. Key bakery considerations:

  • "Use by" dates: For microbiologically high-risk products (custard tarts, cream-filled doughnuts, mousses). Never consume after this date.
  • "Best before" dates: For shelf-stable bakery items (bread, cookies, muffins without cream filling). Quality may decline after this date, but the food is still safe.
  • Baked fresh daily: If you sell unpackaged bread or pastries that are freshly baked and intended for consumption within 12 hours, you may not need a date mark — but you must have a system for removing unsold products at the end of the day.
  • Labelling accuracy: Your shelf life determinations must be scientifically justified. If you claim a 5-day shelf life for a product, you must have storage trials or validated microbiological data to support it. Underestimating shelf life leads to waste; overestimating it risks food poisoning.

Temperature Control for Dairy and Eggs in Patisserie Operations

Dairy products, eggs, and egg-based preparations are central to patisserie and are the highest-risk ingredients in your kitchen. Temperature control requirements are strict:

  • Egg storage: Eggs must be stored at or below 5°C (or as specified by the supplier). Do not wash eggs before storage — washing removes the natural protective cuticle and increases contamination risk.
  • Dairy products: Milk, cream, butter, cream cheese, and mascarpone must be stored at ≤ 5°C at all times. Monitor and record temperatures at least twice daily.
  • Egg wash: If you prepare and use egg wash throughout the day, replace it every 2 hours (or sooner if the ambient temperature is warm). Never top up — start fresh.
  • Custards and crème patissière: These are potentially hazardous foods. Cook to a minimum of 75°C, cool rapidly (60°C to 21°C within 2 hours, then to 5°C within a further 4 hours), and store chilled at ≤ 5°C. Use within 24–48 hours maximum.
  • Buttercreams and icings: Dairy-based buttercreams must be refrigerated. Remove from refrigeration only for the time needed for decorating (typically no more than 30 minutes). Return any unused icing to the fridge immediately.
  • Display cabinets: Refrigerated display cabinets must maintain ≤ 5°C. Check the actual temperature of the products (not just the air temperature) using a probe thermometer. Open-front cabinets lose cold air rapidly — staff should minimise door openings and check product temperatures every 2 hours.

Waste Management and Nuisance Pests in Bakeries

Bakeries produce significant organic waste — flour dust, dough scraps, sugar residues — that attracts pests. Specific controls include:

  • Flour dust extraction and ventilation systems must be cleaned regularly to prevent build-up.
  • Waste bins must be emptied at least daily and stored away from production areas.
  • Dough scraps should not be left in open bins overnight (they ferment and attract flies).
  • Bait stations and insect light traps must be positioned away from food production areas but in locations that intercept pest pathways.
  • Flour and sugar storage must be in sealed containers — weevils and pantry moths are a persistent risk in bakeries.

Audit Preparation for Bakeries and Patisseries

When the NSW Food Authority or your local council inspects your bakery, they will focus on:

  • Temperature control of high-risk ingredients and finished products (especially dairy and egg-based items).
  • Allergen management — procedures, staff knowledge, and evidence of controls.
  • Date marking compliance on both pre-packaged and displayed products.
  • Cleaning and sanitation — particularly of decorating equipment, piping bags, and display cabinets.
  • Pest control — flour mills, sugar containers, and dry goods storage are prime inspection points.
  • Hand washing frequency — bakery staff handling dough, eggs, and finished product must wash hands between each task.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Does my bakery need a separate Food Safety Program for gluten-free products?
Not a separate program, but your existing Food Safety Program must include specific controls for gluten-free production. This covers ingredient verification (certified gluten-free suppliers), segregation of gluten-free ingredients and equipment, cleaning validation (you may need gluten-specific swab testing), and labelling. If you claim "gluten-free" on a product, you must comply with Standard 1.2.7 (Nutrition, Health and Related Claims) and Standard 1.2.3 (Mandatory Labelling). The gluten-free claim is strictly regulated — the product must contain no detectable gluten (≤ 3 mg/100 g for "gluten free" claim).
Can I display cream cakes and custard tarts at room temperature?
No, not in NSW unless you have a validated, Food Safety Program-approved alternative. Cream, custard, and dairy-based fillings are potentially hazardous foods. They must be displayed at ≤ 5°C in a refrigerated cabinet. Ambient display of these products is a common — and serious — non-conformance in bakery audits. If your cabinet cannot maintain temperature, have it serviced or replaced before your next inspection.
How long can I keep egg wash at room temperature during service?
Egg wash is a potentially hazardous food. It must not be held at temperatures between 5°C and 60°C for more than 2 hours. In a busy bakery, we recommend replacing egg wash every 1–2 hours and never topping up old egg wash with fresh. Prepare only the quantity you will use within that window. Discard any unused egg wash at the end of service and clean the container thoroughly. Record this in your cleaning schedule.
