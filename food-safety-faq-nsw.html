Food Safety FAQ for NSW Businesses

Answers to the most common food safety questions from NSW cafe owners, restaurant operators, and food business managers — written by a food technologist with 11+ years at The Arnott's Group.

HACCP Plans & Food Safety Programs

What is a HACCP plan and do I need one in NSW?
HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is a systematic approach to identifying and managing food safety hazards in food production. In NSW, Class 2 food businesses — which includes most cafes, restaurants, caterers, and food manufacturers — are legally required to maintain a documented Food Safety Program based on HACCP principles under Standard 3.2.1 of the Food Standards Code. The program must be available for inspection by NSW Food Authority officers.
What is the difference between a HACCP plan and a Food Safety Program?
HACCP is the methodology — the systematic approach to identifying and managing food safety hazards. A Food Safety Program (FSP) is the legal document you are required to maintain under Standard 3.2.1. Your FSP is built using HACCP principles, but a HACCP plan alone is not a complete FSP — it must also include all eight elements required by Standard 3.2.1, be reviewed annually, and be available for inspection.
Can I use a free HACCP template I found online?
You can use a template as a starting point, but a generic template is not a compliant Food Safety Program. Standard 3.2.1 requires your FSP to be specific to your food business — your menu, your equipment, your staff, and your processes. A hazard analysis that lists "biological hazards" without identifying which specific pathogens are relevant to your menu is not a compliant hazard analysis. AMES Food Advisory builds every HACCP plan from a site visit and thorough review of your specific operation.
How often does my Food Safety Program need to be reviewed?
Your Food Safety Program must be reviewed at least annually and whenever a significant change occurs — new menu items, new equipment, change of premises, changes to food safety regulations, or following a food safety incident. There is no fixed expiry on a well-maintained FSP, but one that has not been reviewed for more than 12 months is almost certainly out of date and may not reflect your current operation.
What are Critical Control Points (CCPs)?
A Critical Control Point is a step in your food handling process where a control measure can be applied to prevent, eliminate, or reduce a food safety hazard to an acceptable level. For a typical Sydney cafe, CCPs commonly include cooking poultry and eggs to a minimum core temperature of 75°C, cooling cooked foods from 60°C to 21°C within 2 hours, and maintaining cold storage at or below 5°C. The specific CCPs for your business depend on your menu and processes.

Food Safety Supervisors (Standard 3.2.2A)

What is a Food Safety Supervisor and does my business need one?
A Food Safety Supervisor (FSS) is a person who holds a current certificate from an approved RTO covering food handling and food safety practices relevant to your business type. Standard 3.2.2A — which fully commenced in December 2023 — requires all food service businesses handling unpackaged potentially hazardous food to have a certified FSS who is reasonably available to supervise food handling operations. This includes cafes, restaurants, caterers, bakeries, and most food manufacturers.
Does the Food Safety Supervisor need to be on-site at all times?
No. Under Standard 3.2.2A, the FSS must be reasonably available to supervise food handling operations — but they do not need to be physically present at all times. They must be contactable during operations and able to provide guidance or intervene when needed. For a small single-site cafe, the FSS will typically be the owner or head chef who is present during most service hours.
What happens if my Food Safety Supervisor leaves the business?
If your FSS leaves the business, their certificate cannot be used — you need a replacement FSS with a current certificate. You should nominate and certify a replacement as quickly as possible. During a NSW Food Authority audit, inspectors will ask to see your FSS certificate details. An absent or lapsed FSS is a non-compliance finding. AMES Food Advisory can help you identify the right certification pathway for your replacement FSS.
How do I notify the NSW Food Authority of my Food Safety Supervisor?
NSW food businesses must notify the NSW Food Authority of their FSS details — including the FSS's name and certificate details. This is done through the NSW Food Authority's online portal. AMES Food Advisory can guide you through the notification process and ensure your documentation is correctly completed.

Allergen Management

What changed in the 2021 FSANZ allergen amendments?
The 2021 FSANZ amendments introduced three key changes: sesame was elevated to mandatory major allergen status (from February 2024), strengthened advisory statement requirements were introduced (requiring "may contain" statements to reflect a genuine assessed cross-contact risk), and lupin was confirmed as a mandatory major allergen. For Sydney cafes and restaurants using sesame-topped bread, tahini, dukkah, or sesame oil, sesame cross-contact is now a documented food safety requirement.
Do I need to list allergens on my menu?
There is no requirement under the Food Standards Code to print a full allergen declaration on a cafe or restaurant menu. However, Standard 3.2.2 requires food businesses to be able to provide allergen information to customers on request. Best practice is to have an allergen matrix available for staff to reference when customers ask. For businesses with a fixed menu, some operators now include abbreviated allergen information on the menu itself.
What is the difference between allergen cross-contact and cross-contamination?
Cross-contamination in food safety typically refers to the transfer of microbiological hazards (bacteria, viruses) between foods or surfaces. Cross-contact refers specifically to the unintentional transfer of allergens from one food to another through shared equipment, surfaces, utensils, or food handlers. Cross-contact can occur even when equipment appears visually clean — residual allergen protein may remain after washing that is insufficient to cause microbiological concern but enough to trigger an allergic reaction.

NSW Food Authority Audits & Inspections

What does a NSW Food Authority inspector look for?
NSW Food Authority inspectors assess food businesses against the requirements of the Food Act 2003 and the Food Standards Code. Key areas include: the currency and completeness of your Food Safety Program, evidence that monitoring procedures are being followed (temperature logs, corrective action records), Food Safety Supervisor certificate, staff food safety knowledge, food storage temperatures, personal hygiene, and premises cleanliness. AMES Food Advisory can conduct a pre-audit gap assessment to identify compliance issues before an inspector arrives.
What happens if I fail a NSW Food Authority audit?
The response to a failed audit depends on the severity of the non-compliance. Minor issues may result in a written notice requiring corrective action within a specified timeframe. More serious issues may result in a prohibition order, improvement notice, or penalty infringement notice. In severe cases, a business can be ordered to close. AMES Food Advisory can help you respond to audit findings and implement the required corrective actions.
How often does the NSW Food Authority inspect food businesses?
Inspection frequency depends on your food business classification and compliance history. Class 1 businesses (serving vulnerable populations) are inspected more frequently than Class 2 businesses. Businesses with a history of non-compliance may be inspected more often. In addition to scheduled inspections, the NSW Food Authority can conduct unannounced inspections at any time in response to complaints or food safety incidents.

Training & Certification

What food safety training do my staff need?
Standard 3.2.2A requires all food handlers to have skills and knowledge appropriate to their work. For kitchen staff, this means understanding the food safety hazards relevant to their tasks — temperature control, personal hygiene, allergen awareness, and how to follow your Food Safety Program. Food safety induction training should be provided to all new food handling staff before they work unsupervised. Refresher training is recommended at least annually.
Does AMES Food Advisory deliver on-site training?
Yes. All AMES Food Advisory training programs can be delivered on-site at your premises. On-site delivery allows training to be contextualised to your actual kitchen layout, equipment, and menu — which is typically more effective than generic classroom-based training. Training records suitable for inclusion in your Food Safety Program documentation are provided for all training delivered by AMES.
